Effective accelerationism has a simple theory of progress: constraints slow technology down, so remove
them. Let the market move as fast as it can.

The problem is that this misunderstands what a constraint is. As I argued in
[A Constraint Theory of Technology](/essays/a-constraint-theory-of-technology), constraints are not just
limits. They also enable. And crucially, they don't go away when you ignore them. Technologies that emerge
from an unconstrained market still meet limits; those limits just arrive later, downstream of the market.
They become "reactive instead of proactive." And "limits like public backlash or legal challenges or
political regulation are going to be much harsher precisely because they are reactive."

That is what makes accelerationism self-defeating, on its own terms. The argument isn't that acceleration
is dangerous or immoral. It's that the strategy fails at the one thing it wants: speed.

## Every constraint layer pushes back

Technology is always constrained by many layers at once. Some are foundational: religion, philosophy,
and the planet itself. Others are situational: the state, culture, ethics, and our own personal limits.
The market is only one layer among them.

When the market refuses to constrain itself, it doesn't escape the others. It provokes them, all at once:

- **The state** responds with regulation written in a hurry, after the harm, by people who feel they were
  ignored.
- **Culture** responds with culture wars: technology becomes a tribal identity marker rather than a tool.
- **Ethics** responds with moral panic and public distrust, which then feeds back into the state and culture.

As the essay put it, unconstrained technologies "will increasingly become targets of culture wars, virtue
signaling, and political regulation." Each of these layers is slower, blunter and more repressive than any
constraint the market could have set for itself. A proactive constraint is a dial. A reactive one is a
wall.

## The accelerationist's best move is to constrain

The irony is that the market has the most to gain from constraining itself. "The more constraints the
market can provide, the less need there is for outside constraints to intervene." Red teaming, safe-to-fail
probes and voluntary limits aren't concessions to the critics of acceleration. They are the conditions that
keep the other layers from slamming the brakes.

"So by wanting to remove all constraints, the optimists are actually removing the only effective means of
acceleration."

## Open questions

- Nuclear power may be the clearest case: an industry that outran public trust and then spent decades
  stalled by backlash and regulation. Does AI follow the same path?
- How does this relate to the Collingridge dilemma, that a technology is easiest to steer when we know
  least about its effects?
- What would a constraint look like that accelerationists would *want*, because it buys speed?
