# Ideas in "Homo Digitalis"

Framed by the vignettes of two digital natives, Maya (who avoids a phone call and a delivery driver) and Alex (a livestreamer afraid of a physical date), the essay combines the free energy principle with Roberto Mangabeira Unger's dialectic of finitude and transcendence to argue that digital immersion is producing a new kind of human. It holds nine distinct ideas.

## Digital natives are a new type of human defined by their relationship to uncertainty
- **Type:** reframe
- **Claim:** Griggs contends that the struggles of digital natives with phone calls or physical dates reflect neither immaturity nor a lack of responsibility, but a fundamentally different relationship with uncertainty, which in turn shapes which experiences, including transcendent ones, they are capable of.
- **In the essay:** The thesis, set up by the opening vignettes and the observation that the same people may responsibly manage online communities of thousands. Anchor: "It's about being a fundamentally new type of human being, one shaped by a radically different relationship with uncertainty."
- **Stands alone because:** It offers an alternative to "kids these days" and pure screen-time-harm explanations of Gen Z social anxiety.
- **Micro-article:** "It's Not Immaturity: Why Digital Natives Fear Phone Calls" · "Why are young people so anxious about phone calls and in-person interactions?" · (1) The paradox: high online responsibility, offline paralysis; (2) the reframe: a different uncertainty profile, not a deficit of maturity; (3) why it matters: uncertainty governs access to transcendence.
- **Prior art to engage:** Jonathan Haidt's *The Anxious Generation* (afterbabel is linked in a companion essay, not here); not cited in this essay: Sherry Turkle (*Alone Together*, *Reclaiming Conversation*), Jean Twenge (*iGen*), Prensky's "digital natives."
- **Connections:** what-does-a-good-digital-life-look, the-question-concerning-digital-technology
- **Strength:** 3

## Phone-call anxiety explained by active inference
- **Type:** argument
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that, under the free energy principle, the phone call produces anxiety because it carries many unmodeled sources of uncertainty (immediacy, no editing, unpredictable topic, tone that leaks intention) compared with asynchronous chat; faced with that, active inference allows either acting or learning, and declining the call is the cheapest action that reduces uncertainty.
- **In the essay:** The section "The free energy principle in action" works through a side-by-side comparison of a chat message and a phone call. Anchor: "The anxiety Maya feels when the phone rings is her brain trying to convince her to minimize uncertainty."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a clear, teachable application of active inference to an everyday, widely discussed behavior.
- **Micro-article:** "The Neuroscience of Not Answering the Phone" · "What does the free energy principle say about why people avoid phone calls?" · (1) The free energy principle in plain terms; (2) chat vs. call as low vs. high uncertainty; (3) act vs. learn: why voicemail wins, and how practice could change the model.
- **Prior art to engage:** Friston's free energy principle (linked). Not cited: Andy Clark's *Surfing Uncertainty*, predictive-processing accounts of anxiety, the telephobia literature.
- **Connections:** infinite-dimensionality (active inference), towards-a-philosophy-of-technology
- **Strength:** 2

## The moon-born human: digital environments change the rules of uncertainty itself
- **Type:** thought experiment
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that ordinary differences in handling uncertainty (the traveler, the musician, the tracker) are like differences in strength within the same gravity, whereas digital natives are like someone raised on the moon: the environmental variable that varies is uncertainty itself, so they follow an entirely different rulebook.
- **In the essay:** The section "A different uncertainty rulebook," which returns at the end ("Like muscles that normalize on the moon"). Anchor: "the variation is in uncertainty itself."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a vivid, portable analogy that separates individual variation from environmental variation, useful in any discussion of digital development.
- **Micro-article:** "Raised on the Moon: Why Digital Natives Play by a Different Uncertainty Rulebook" · "Are digital natives just worse at handling uncertainty, or is it something different?" · (1) Variation within one environment (strength, expertise); (2) the moon thought experiment; (3) digital worlds vary uncertainty itself, so physical reality feels like the wrong gravity.
- **Prior art to engage:** Not cited in the essay. Developmental niche construction, Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems, Gibson's affordances.
- **Connections:** what-does-a-good-digital-life-look
- **Strength:** 3

## Digital environments: all choice and no circumstance
- **Type:** concept
- **Claim:** Griggs characterizes digital environments by their "radical submission to our will": attention and intention nearly coincide, content and social circles are curated, the hard facts of history, reputation, appearance, and even selfhood become tunable dials, and remaining uncertainty comes in fast, contained, controllable micro-cycles (notifications, swipes, refreshes) that train the brain to expect quick, clean resolution.
- **In the essay:** The longest section, "Normalizing digital uncertainty." Anchor: "It's all choice and no circumstance."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a phenomenology of digital space that explains both its appeal and why physical reality, by contrast, "pushes back."
- **Micro-article:** "All Choice, No Circumstance: The Phenomenology of Digital Control" · "How do online environments differ psychologically from the physical world?" · (1) Attention equals intention, and constraints become dials (including the self); (2) micro-uncertainty loops and contained high stakes (mute, block, delete); (3) normalization: the brain's training set, and why physical interaction feels like "wildfire."
- **Prior art to engage:** Not cited in the essay. Variable reward schedules (Skinner, Nir Eyal's *Hooked*), Eli Pariser's filter bubble, Turkle on identity play, Goffman on self-presentation, Albert Borgmann's device paradigm.
- **Connections:** the-question-concerning-digital-technology, what-does-a-good-digital-life-look
- **Strength:** 3

## Finitude and transcendence are co-constitutive
- **Type:** theory
- **Claim:** Following Unger, Griggs holds that finitude and transcendence give each other shape, so that the quality and quantity of transcendence available is proportional to the finitude one is willing to confront: love's depth carries the risk of loss, and facing death brings more life.
- **In the essay:** The section "Finite transcendence," illustrated with love, Pascal's sun image, athletes, artists, entrepreneurs, and cross-cultural aphorisms ("No pain, no gain"). Anchor: "Finitude is a structural feature of how transcendence works."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a general account of meaning that can be applied to technology, design, and ethics, and it is attributable even though it originates with Unger.
- **Micro-article:** "No Finitude, No Transcendence: Unger's Dialectic for the Digital Age" · "Why does meaningful experience seem to require risk, limits, and loss?" · (1) Unger's two poles; (2) co-constitution and proportionality, with examples; (3) why this makes our capacity to face uncertainty decisive.
- **Prior art to engage:** Roberto Mangabeira Unger, *The World and Us* (cited); Pascal (cited). Not cited: Heidegger's being-toward-death, Kierkegaard, Becker's *The Denial of Death*, Hägglund's *This Life*.
- **Connections:** life-is-special-enough (finitude fuels value), the-plurality-a-better-myth-for-ai, what-does-a-good-digital-life-look (Unger)
- **Strength:** 3

## Platforms hijack the dialectic: all transcendence, none of the finitude
- **Type:** critique
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that digital platforms sell transcendence without its corresponding finitude ("community without commitment, connection without consequence, sex without rejection"), as if capitalism found a shortcut through the dialectic; for adults raised before digital media this is a hijacking, and the delivery eventually feels hollow.
- **In the essay:** The section "All transcendence, none of the finitude," which first acknowledges real digital transcendence (global connection, collective projects) before the critique. Anchor: "all the transcendence, none of the finitude."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a crisp, quotable critique of the attention economy grounded in a theory of meaning rather than in harm metrics.
- **Micro-article:** "All Transcendence, None of the Finitude: The Shortcut at the Heart of Digital Platforms" · "Why do online connection and community often feel empty?" · (1) Real digital transcendence, conceded; (2) the shortcut and its commercial logic; (3) why meaning unmoored from limits becomes hard to tell from empty engagement.
- **Prior art to engage:** Unger (cited). Not cited: Turkle, Byung-Chul Han (*The Agony of Eros*, *The Transparency Society*), Borgmann's focal practices, Zuboff.
- **Connections:** the-question-concerning-digital-technology, what-does-a-good-digital-life-look, life-is-special-enough
- **Strength:** 3

## Hijacking vs. normalizing the shortcut
- **Type:** distinction
- **Claim:** Griggs distinguishes how the transcendence shortcut affects those raised before digital media (it hijacks an existing "no pain, no gain" foundation) from how it affects digital natives (it is normalized, so transcendence is expected to be easy and achieving a desire is assumed to be as simple as expressing it); the second makes physical transcendence, such as vulnerable physical intimacy, harder to pursue, which he links to reports of young people having less sex.
- **In the essay:** The second half of "All transcendence, none of the finitude." Anchor: "Instead of hijacking the traditional dialectic between finitude and transcendence, it's normalizing the shortcut."
- **Stands alone because:** It explains generational differences in the effects of the same technology with one variable: whether a pre-digital foundation exists.
- **Micro-article:** "Hijacked vs. Normalized: Why the Same Apps Affect Generations Differently" · "Why are digital natives having less sex and fewer in-person relationships?" · (1) The hijack for older users; (2) normalization for natives: transcendence expected to be effortless; (3) vulnerability as an uncertainty they never learned to inhabit (the sex-recession example).
- **Prior art to engage:** KFF Health News on declining youth sex (linked). Not cited: Twenge on the "sex recession," Haidt, Kate Julian's *Atlantic* reporting.
- **Connections:** what-does-a-good-digital-life-look
- **Strength:** 2

## AI companions as the logical conclusion of the shortcut
- **Type:** critique
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that AI chatbot companions perfect the shortcut, offering care and listening with no demand for reciprocity and no change, growth, or challenge without consent; he does not claim they offer nothing, but warns that they narrow the range of possible transcendence and weaken the capacity to expand it.
- **In the essay:** A paragraph within "All transcendence, none of the finitude," with a careful caveat after it. Anchor: "It's the perfect embodiment of digital certainty—a relationship promising all transcendence without any corresponding finitude."
- **Stands alone because:** AI companionship is a live public question, and this offers a principled, non-alarmist criterion: what is missing is finitude, not authenticity.
- **Micro-article:** "Why AI Companions Can't Give You What Relationships Give: The Missing Finitude" · "Are relationships with AI chatbots bad for you?" · (1) What AI companions offer and omit (reciprocity, challenge, change); (2) the finitude-transcendence lens; (3) the real risk: narrowed possibility and atrophied capacity, not inherent badness.
- **Prior art to engage:** Not cited in the essay. Turkle's *Alone Together*, research on Replika and Character.AI users, Buber's I-Thou, Shannon Vallor.
- **Connections:** schrodingers-chatbot, what-does-a-good-digital-life-look, the-reverse-turing-test
- **Strength:** 3

## Unchosen foreclosure and the reverse hijack
- **Type:** argument
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that the narrowing of human possibility through digital immersion "just happens" rather than being chosen, because the brain minimizes uncertainty within whatever environment it lives in; the capacity to confront finitude atrophies, so that for digital natives the physical world becomes a reverse hijack, "all finitude, with none of the transcendence." The live questions are whether we can develop meta-awareness to move between worlds, and whether a free, fully informed choice of digital immersion should be accepted without judgment.
- **In the essay:** End of "All transcendence, none of the finitude" and the closing section "Adapting to Our New Reality(ies)," which leaves the equilibrium questions open. Anchors: "this foreclosure of possibility just happens" and "all finitude, with none of the transcendence."
- **Stands alone because:** It moves the debate from individual choice and blame to environmental shaping, and it frames freedom as an understanding of how technology changes our own nature.
- **Micro-article:** "Nobody Chose This: How Digital Immersion Quietly Narrows Human Possibility" · "Is digital immersion something people choose, and should we judge those who prefer it?" · (1) Atrophy: muscles normalized on the moon; (2) the physical world as reverse hijack; (3) open questions: meta-awareness, equilibrium, and accepting free choices without judgment.
- **Prior art to engage:** Heidegger on technology and being (the series' first essay, not cited here). Not cited: Amartya Sen's capabilities, adaptive preferences (Elster's *Sour Grapes*), Nussbaum.
- **Connections:** the-question-concerning-digital-technology (Heidegger and the essence of technology; series part one), what-does-a-good-digital-life-look (series part two)
- **Strength:** 2
