# Ideas in "On the Moral Natures of Humans and Machines"

Framed around a self-driving car that hits a child, and then the real 2023 Cruise pedestrian-dragging incident, the essay argues that autonomous machines are becoming moral peers whose foreign moral natures disrupt the shared background of human moral life. It holds nine distinct ideas, several of them coined concepts (confusion tax, moral arena, moral terms of engagement).

## The confusion tax
- **Type:** concept
- **Claim:** Griggs holds that when technology outruns our vocabulary we are forced to co-opt human terms like "intelligence" or "creativity," and we pay a "confusion tax": we both anthropomorphize machines and fail to see what is genuinely novel about them.
- **In the essay:** Introduced in its own section ("Paying the confusion tax") and developed in two directions, over-attribution (assuming a chat interface has intentions) and under-recognition (missing novelty that departs from human conceptions). Anchor: "It's the price we pay when technology exceeds the vocabulary we have to describe it."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a general diagnosis of how people talk about AI, and it applies to any debate over whether AI "thinks," "understands," or "creates," with no need for the car example.
- **Micro-article:** "The Confusion Tax: Why Describing AI in Human Words Costs Us Twice" · "Why is it misleading to call AI intelligent or creative?" · (1) Faced with the unknown, our only move is to co-opt the known; (2) the double cost: anthropomorphism plus blindness to novelty; (3) what to do meanwhile: notice the limits of co-opted terms until a new lexicon exists.
- **Prior art to engage:** Not cited in the essay. Relates to Wittgenstein on language games, Dennett's intentional stance, Lakoff and Johnson on metaphor, the "ELIZA effect," and Bender et al. on "stochastic parrots" and anthropomorphic framing.
- **Connections:** schrodingers-chatbot (the subject/object ambiguity of chatbots), the-majesty-of-language, the-reverse-turing-test
- **Strength:** 3

## Moral vocabulary pushes the confusion tax into a "new tax bracket"
- **Type:** argument
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that confusion is worse for moral terms like "forgiveness," "dignity," and "trust" than for cognitive ones, because moral confusion distorts not only how we perceive machines but how we actually interact with them and with each other.
- **In the essay:** The section "Welcome to our new tax bracket" extends the confusion tax to moral language and claims AMAs "threaten to disrupt the shared understanding of moral concepts that our ethical frameworks depend on." Anchor: "Autonomous moral agents are pushing us into an entirely new tax bracket of confusion."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a claim about the stakes of terminology in AI ethics: a misapplied moral word changes behavior, not just belief.
- **Micro-article:** "Why Moral Words Break Harder Than Cognitive Words When Applied to AI" · "Can you forgive or trust a machine in the same sense you forgive a person?" · (1) Moral terms are grounded in subjective experience; (2) misapplying them changes interactions and erodes shared moral understanding; (3) the risk runs both ways: we may also fail to recognize genuinely new machine moral frameworks.
- **Prior art to engage:** Not cited in the essay. Moral-patiency and machine-ethics debates (Floridi and Sanders on artificial agency, Coeckelbergh's relational approach), Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment."
- **Connections:** schrodingers-chatbot
- **Strength:** 2

## Autonomous moral agents as moral peers
- **Type:** concept
- **Claim:** Griggs defines autonomous moral agents (AMAs) functionally, as agents that take similar inputs and produce comparable morally weighty outputs to human agents, and holds that even with radically different moral natures they are becoming our "moral peers," a new relationship to technology.
- **In the essay:** Set up right after the opening thought experiment; it frames the whole piece. Anchor: "Even if their moral natures are radically different from ours, these AMAs are becoming our moral peers."
- **Stands alone because:** It offers a functional, consciousness-neutral definition of moral agency in machines that can be used in any AI ethics discussion.
- **Micro-article:** "Moral Peers Without Moral Minds: A Functional Definition of Autonomous Moral Agents" · "Are self-driving cars and AI agents moral agents?" · (1) The functional definition: comparable inputs, outputs, real-time decisions with moral weight; (2) why differing moral nature does not stop peer status; (3) the new questions it raises: shared standards, empathy, trust, adoption.
- **Prior art to engage:** Not cited in the essay. Wallach and Allen (*Moral Machines*), Moor's typology of ethical agents, Floridi and Sanders (2004) on "mindless morality".
- **Connections:** calling-all-philosophers (AV dilemma questions), schrodingers-chatbot
- **Strength:** 2

## Intersubjective morality: a third category beyond ethics and morals
- **Type:** distinction
- **Claim:** Griggs proposes that beside ethics (objective, collective standards) and morals (subjective, individual practices) there is an intersubjective layer of shared understandings and mutual expectations between agents, and that this layer is what machines disrupt.
- **In the essay:** The section "Beyond ethics vs. morals" draws the three-way distinction and illustrates intersubjectivity with the tacit trust between human drivers (shared training, shared fallibility, mutual dignity, knowing when rules can be broken). Anchor: "intersubjectivity is more like the silent background that shapes these interactions".
- **Stands alone because:** It is a reusable framework for any human-machine ethics question and names what rule-based AI ethics tends to leave out.
- **Micro-article:** "Ethics, Morals, and the Missing Third Layer: Intersubjectivity in Human-Machine Interaction" · "What is intersubjective trust, and why do AI and self-driving cars disrupt it?" · (1) Ethics vs. morals as usually drawn; (2) intersubjectivity as the silent background of shared nature and mutual expectation, shown through driving; (3) why agents with foreign moral natures break that background even when they follow the rules.
- **Prior art to engage:** Not cited in the essay. Husserl and Schutz on intersubjectivity, Habermas on communicative action, Searle on social reality, Annette Baier on trust.
- **Connections:** schrodingers-chatbot (refers back to this essay's "new intersubjective reality"), what-does-a-good-digital-life-look
- **Strength:** 3

## Forbearance replaces forgiveness when a machine harms you
- **Type:** argument
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that when an autonomous vehicle causes harm, responsibility dissolves into a "fog," empathy is blocked by the machine's foreign nature, and forgiveness becomes nearly meaningless, so victims are left only with forbearance: tolerating the harm for the sake of an aggregate greater good, a utilitarian bargain that feels hollow in personal tragedy.
- **In the essay:** The section "How to forgive a machine" sets responsibility, empathy, and forgiveness toward a human driver against each one toward an AV. Anchor: "In place of forgiveness, there is only forbearance."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a precise, quotable claim about the moral psychology of harm by machines, relevant to AV policy, medical AI, and algorithmic decisions generally.
- **Micro-article:** "Can You Forgive a Self-Driving Car? Why Machine Harm Leaves Only Forbearance" · "How should victims feel about harm caused by an autonomous vehicle, and can they forgive it?" · (1) Responsibility, empathy, forgiveness under a human driver; (2) what happens to each under an AV; (3) forbearance and the hollowness of statistical consolation, plus the call for new frameworks of coexistence.
- **Prior art to engage:** Not cited in the essay. Matthias's "responsibility gap," Strawson's reactive attitudes, Santoni de Sio and Mecacci on responsibility gaps, the ethics of forgiveness (Griswold), Bernard Williams's critiques of utilitarianism.
- **Connections:** calling-all-philosophers
- **Strength:** 3

## Trust requires dignity, not only safety (the Cruise lesson)
- **Type:** argument
- **Claim:** Griggs argues, using the 2023 Cruise incident, that trust in autonomous agents has two parts, functional reliability and confidence that the agent will respect human dignity, and that a dehumanizing outcome can override aggregate safety arguments.
- **In the essay:** The section "Trust isn't just about safety" notes that Cruise halted operations over an outcome seen as dehumanizing, which was not a functional failure, even though the maneuver was designed for safety. Anchor: "It's another thing entirely to trust an AV to respect our dignity as moral agents."
- **Stands alone because:** It is grounded in a real, documented case and offers a design and policy lesson that holds beyond AVs.
- **Micro-article:** "Statistically Safe, Morally Unacceptable: What the Cruise Dragging Incident Teaches About Trust in AI" · "Why did Cruise shut down after the pedestrian-dragging incident if its cars were safe overall?" · (1) The incident and the safety logic behind the maneuver; (2) dignity as the minimum respect owed, and why the spectacle violated it; (3) trust = reliability + dignity, with implications for deployment.
- **Prior art to engage:** Not cited in the essay beyond Phil Koopman's case write-up (linked). Kantian dignity, research on trust in automation (Lee and See), the "uncanny" or dehumanization literature.
- **Connections:** the-high-dimensional-society and the-question-concerning-digital-technology (dignity), calling-all-philosophers
- **Strength:** 3

## Intersubjective harms must not be outweighed just because ethics is quantifiable
- **Type:** design principle
- **Claim:** Griggs holds that intersubjective considerations deserve equal weight with traditional ethical arguments, and that aggregate benefits like fewer total fatalities should not automatically override extreme intersubjective violations simply because the former can be measured and the latter cannot.
- **In the essay:** Stated as the second requirement in "Defining the moral terms of engagement." Anchor: "The fact that ethical arguments can be quantified and analyzed in ways that intersubjective arguments cannot should not be allowed to tip the scales on their moral impact."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a general critique of quantification bias in tech ethics and cost-benefit governance.
- **Micro-article:** "Against Quantification Bias in AI Ethics" · "Should lower overall fatality rates justify deploying self-driving cars no matter what?" · (1) The asymmetry: aggregate outcomes are measurable, intersubjective violations are not; (2) why measurability is not moral weight; (3) how to give intersubjective harms equal standing in adoption decisions.
- **Prior art to engage:** Not cited in the essay. McNamara fallacy, Goodhart's law, Sen on incommensurability, critiques of cost-benefit analysis (Anderson, Ackerman and Heinzerling).
- **Connections:** the-high-dimensional-society (quantity over quality), infinite-dimensionality ("quality as quantity" as a low-dimensional paradigm), a-constraint-theory-of-technology (price signal)
- **Strength:** 2

## Moral arenas and the incompleteness of human-machine alignment
- **Type:** theory
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that every intersubjective system of rules and norms, which he calls a "moral arena," is designed for a particular moral nature; since traffic, law, and corporate structures are built for contextual, normative, ambiguous, social humans, machines placed in them will always leave a gap, an incompleteness argument against 100% alignment, so the real question is how much misalignment to tolerate.
- **In the essay:** The section "Moral arenas" lists four traits of human traffic (contextual, normative, ambiguous, social) and invokes Gödel by analogy. Anchors: "This ambiguity is a feature, not a bug." and "how much misalignment are we willing to tolerate?"
- **Stands alone because:** It recasts AI alignment as a fit problem between an agent and its environment rather than a property of the agent alone, and it applies to any AI deployed into human institutions.
- **Micro-article:** "Moral Arenas: Why AI Can Never Be Fully Aligned With Systems Built for Humans" · "Why can't self-driving cars just follow traffic rules perfectly?" · (1) Moral arenas are designed for a moral nature; (2) four human traits baked into traffic (eye contact, "reasonable behavior," rule-breaking for context); (3) the irreducible gap and the shift from perfect alignment to tolerable misalignment.
- **Prior art to engage:** Gödel's incompleteness theorems (linked, used as analogy). Not cited: Hart on open texture in law, Polanyi on tacit knowledge, Dreyfus's critique of AI, Suchman on situated action, the "specification gaming" literature.
- **Connections:** a-constraint-theory-of-technology, the-high-dimensional-society
- **Strength:** 3

## Adaptation, separation, coevolution: three ways to adopt autonomous agents
- **Type:** design principle
- **Claim:** Griggs holds that the moral terms of engagement with AMAs depend on a design choice of arena, with three options: force machines to adapt to human environments, build separate controlled environments for them, or coevolve hybrid spaces; he judges coevolution most likely to succeed long term but the most demanding, and asks that moral implications be weighed alongside cost, utility, and feasibility.
- **In the essay:** The final requirement of "Defining the moral terms of engagement," along with the claim that moral terms become more contextual (dignity may mean different things in different arenas). Anchor: "Ethics cannot be a mere afterthought."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a practical decision framework for policymakers and designers deploying any autonomous system.
- **Micro-article:** "Adapt, Separate, or Coevolve? Three Strategies for Living With Autonomous Machines" · "Should we redesign roads and institutions for AI, or make AI fit ours?" · (1) The moral terms of engagement as explicit design inputs; (2) the three strategies with their trade-offs; (3) context-dependent moral terms and the closing call to widen the moral "we."
- **Prior art to engage:** Not cited in the essay. Dedicated AV lanes and geofencing debates, value-sensitive design (Friedman), Latour on delegation to nonhumans, moral-circle expansion (Singer).
- **Connections:** the-plurality-a-better-myth-for-ai (coevolution with AI), calling-all-philosophers
- **Strength:** 2
