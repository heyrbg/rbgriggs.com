# Ideas in "Our Future with Cognitive Enhancement"

A plain-language list of ten philosophical implications of cognitive enhancement (from wearables to BCIs to a full merge with machines). Several of the ten sections contain more than one separable idea, for 13 distinct ideas in all.

## Thought compression is lossy, and the loss can reshape thinking
- **Type:** argument
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that any "digital unit of thought" a BCI transmits must compress cognition with loss, as language already does, and that a brain shaped by its environment may let unused parts of cognition wither as it adapts to the standardized unit.
- **In the essay:** Section 1, "How to compress a thought." Anchor: "Something inherent to human thought will always be left out."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a basic objection to the "bandwidth" pitch for BCIs, and it holds whatever device is involved.
- **Micro-article:** "Is There an MP3 for Thought? The Lossy Compression Problem in Brain-Computer Interfaces" · "What gets lost when thoughts are transmitted through a brain-computer interface?" ·
  - Language is already lossy compression of thought
  - BCIs will transmit only the parts their goals need
  - Plasticity means the format can reshape the mind, for better (a new style, like in painting) or worse (atrophy)
- **Prior art to engage:** McLuhan ("the medium is the message"); Shannon information theory; Sapir-Whorf (none cited in the essay)
- **Connections:** the-majesty-of-language, the-high-dimensional-society, infinite-dimensionality, the-reverse-turing-test
- **Strength:** 3

## The self as pure artifice
- **Type:** prediction
- **Claim:** Griggs predicts that cognitive enhancement will dissolve the boundary between the inner self and outside tools, so identity becomes something actively assembled from altered memories, downloads and collectives one joins, carrying online identity fragmentation into the mind itself.
- **In the essay:** Section 2, "Trying on a new self," which builds on online identity play and fragmentation. Anchor: "The traditional divide between the internal construction of the self and its external manifestation may vanish entirely."
- **Stands alone because:** It ties a familiar internet phenomenon to a coming neurotechnology and asks what authenticity means.
- **Micro-article:** "The Self as Pure Artifice: Identity After Cognitive Enhancement" · "How would brain implants change personal identity?" ·
  - Online, identities are already tried on like outfits
  - Enhancement moves that play inside the mind
  - The risks to uniqueness and authenticity, and the chance for self-discovery
- **Prior art to engage:** Sherry Turkle, *Life on the Screen*; Clark and Chalmers's extended mind; Derek Parfit on personal identity (none cited in the essay)
- **Connections:** homo-digitalis, what-does-a-good-digital-life-look
- **Strength:** 2

## Cognitive groupthink: homogenizing feedback loops reach the mind
- **Type:** prediction
- **Claim:** Griggs warns that just as platforms and LLMs (through statistical averaging and RLHF) have flattened the variety of content, brain-linked computation could flatten the variety of thought, unless novelty and randomness are deliberately built in.
- **In the essay:** Section 3. Anchor: "These same outputs become tomorrow’s training data, perpetuating a homogenizing feedback loop."
- **Stands alone because:** Worry about AI homogenization is widespread, and moving it from content to cognition is a short, citable step.
- **Micro-article:** "Will Brain-Computer Interfaces Create a Monoculture of Thought?" · "Could AI and BCIs make everyone think the same?" ·
  - From quirky blogs to a few platforms and "the current thing"
  - LLMs average away unusual inputs; RLHF enforces norms; the outputs feed back as training data
  - The design alternative: reward novelty, inject randomness
- **Prior art to engage:** model collapse research; "algorithmic monoculture" (Kleinberg and Raghavan) (not cited in the essay); Randonautica (footnoted)
- **Connections:** the-high-dimensional-society, the-plurality-a-better-myth-for-ai, the-case-for-immaterial-progress
- **Strength:** 2

## Collective intelligence needs collective wisdom: the alignment problem with ourselves
- **Type:** argument
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that a hive mind would need shared goals, and goals come from values, not facts or intelligence. Values change through a clash between belief and experience rather than through better arguments, so collective intelligence brings an alignment problem with ourselves and may call for moral enhancement. He adds that the same technologies that would empower a hive mind could oppress individual minds.
- **In the essay:** Section 4, "Unlocking the hive mind." Anchor: "Goals are based on values, not facts."
- **Stands alone because:** It applies the AI-alignment frame to human collective intelligence, which is an unusual and useful move.
- **Micro-article:** "The Hive Mind Has an Alignment Problem" · "Would connecting human brains create collective intelligence?" ·
  - Intelligence can't set goals; values do
  - Values change through dissonance with experience, not information
  - Collective wisdom, moral enhancement, and the risk of surveillance and oppression
- **Prior art to engage:** Persson and Savulescu, *Unfit for the Future* (moral bioenhancement); the AI alignment literature; Hume's is/ought gap (none cited in the essay)
- **Connections:** our-planetary-predicament, the-plurality-a-better-myth-for-ai, moral-natures-of-humans-and-machines
- **Strength:** 3

## The real cognitive divide is between adopters and refusers
- **Type:** argument
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that shared enhancements might actually even out intelligence, so the bigger divide will be between those who enhance and those who don't. Economic pressure to enhance could produce a new class politics of "the enhanced" against "the normies," an us-vs-us split rather than the us-vs-them of AI fears.
- **In the essay:** Section 5, "Bridging the cognitive divide," with a footnote that intelligence and happiness correlate at the group level but not the individual level. Anchor: "The bigger risk for a new cognitive divide will be between those that embrace enhancements and those that do not."
- **Stands alone because:** It moves the usual enhancement-inequality debate from access to choice, which is a clear and distinctive position.
- **Micro-article:** "Enhanced vs. Normies: The Cognitive Divide That Actually Matters" · "Will cognitive enhancement create inequality?" ·
  - Why shared enhancements may flatten differences in intelligence
  - Economic pressure turns refusal into a cost
  - Us vs. us: the politics of redistribution and resentment
- **Prior art to engage:** Michael Sandel, *The Case Against Perfection*; Nick Bostrom and transhumanist debates on enhancement equity (not cited in the essay)
- **Connections:** manifesto (opting out), homo-digitalis
- **Strength:** 3

## Enhancement as a speciation event (homo cognito)
- **Type:** prediction
- **Claim:** Griggs speculates that over long timescales the enhanced and the unenhanced could drift apart in values, communication and ways of being until they amount to separate species, and that when two similar species compete for one niche, only one survives.
- **In the essay:** End of section 5; the term "homo cognito" is coined here. Anchor: "The divergence could eventually become so great as to represent a **speciation event**."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a memorable, nameable scenario that people will ask AI about.
- **Micro-article:** "Homo Cognito: Could Cognitive Enhancement Split the Human Species?" · "Could brain enhancement create a new human species?" ·
  - Value drift between the enhanced and unenhanced
  - Ways of communicating and being that no longer fit together
  - Keeping the values that bind the species across the choice to enhance
- **Prior art to engage:** H.G. Wells's Eloi and Morlocks; Yuval Harari's "useless class" and Homo Deus; Fukuyama, *Our Posthuman Future* (none cited in the essay)
- **Connections:** homo-digitalis, moral-natures-of-humans-and-machines
- **Strength:** 2

## Cognitive clock rate and patience as a virtue
- **Type:** concept
- **Claim:** Griggs proposes that the brain's "clock rate" is tuned to the pace of the physical world. Satisficing heuristics trade information for time, and attention is fitted to the senses. Thinking at the speed of thought could put these out of sync, make "meat talk" and long physical projects unbearable, and so make patience newly valuable as a virtue.
- **In the essay:** Section 6, "Living at the speed of thought." Anchor: "Why operate at the speed of atoms when you can operate at the speed of thought?"
- **Stands alone because:** "Clock rate" is a fresh, concrete metaphor for the effect of speed on mind and time horizons, and it already applies to today's media.
- **Micro-article:** "Living at the Speed of Thought: The Clock-Rate Problem" · "What happens to our minds if technology speeds up thinking?" ·
  - Evolved heuristics favor time over information (satisficing)
  - Attention tuned to a faster input finds everything slower unbearable
  - Long-term and physical projects suffer, so patience gains value
- **Prior art to engage:** Herbert Simon on satisficing (linked); Hartmut Rosa on social acceleration; Kahneman's System 1/2 (not cited in the essay)
- **Connections:** the-high-dimensional-society (bits vs. atoms), what-does-a-good-digital-life-look
- **Strength:** 3

## Intelligence is not one dial: enhancement trade-offs
- **Type:** argument
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that "intelligence" is a shorthand for a shifting process among many abilities. Strengthening one (abstract reasoning) may weaken another (empathy, or creativity that depends on the subconscious), so there's no single setting to turn up, and side effects can't be predicted.
- **In the essay:** Section 7, "The complexity of intelligence." Anchor: "abstract reasoning generalizes away the messy details of reality, while empathy must embrace them"
- **Stands alone because:** It is a crisp argument against "just make us smarter," and it fits AI capability debates as well as enhancement.
- **Micro-article:** "There Is No 'Increase Intelligence' Button" · "Can you enhance intelligence without side effects?" ·
  - Intelligence as a process among many aspects, not a quantity
  - Creativity arrives through rest and the subconscious, not more logic
  - Trade-offs (abstraction vs. empathy) and why enhancement's effects are radically uncertain
- **Prior art to engage:** Howard Gardner's multiple intelligences; g-factor debates; incubation research on creativity (not cited in the essay)
- **Connections:** moral-natures-of-humans-and-machines, the-reverse-turing-test
- **Strength:** 2

## Enhancement for connection: empathy and polarization as a resource
- **Type:** reframe
- **Claim:** Griggs suggests that BCIs could let people feel the emotions behind another person's worldview, and could show that political differences come from personality and evolved for a reason. That would turn polarization into a resource for solving problems rather than fuel for culture wars.
- **In the essay:** Section 8, "The cost of connection," his counterweight to the risks above, which also include losing embodied relations. Anchor: "This may help us realize that political differences **evolved for a reason**"
- **Stands alone because:** It is a hopeful, specific design goal (empathy tools, polarization read as diversity), and you don't need the rest of the essay to follow it.
- **Micro-article:** "Could Brain Tech Make Polarization Useful?" · "Could technology increase empathy across political divides?" ·
  - From VR's "through their eyes" to BCIs' "through their thoughts"
  - The psychological roots of political differences
  - Tools for navigating cultural difference that encourage diversity
- **Prior art to engage:** Jonathan Haidt's moral foundations theory; research on personality and political orientation; Paul Bloom, *Against Empathy* (none cited in the essay)
- **Connections:** the-plurality-a-better-myth-for-ai, the-high-dimensional-society
- **Strength:** 2

## Human cognition excels because of its limits
- **Type:** argument
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that the illegible, paradoxical nature of human cognition (logic vs. emotion, reason vs. intuition, conscious vs. subconscious) is where everything we value about it comes from, so the historical habit of swapping the messy for the legible threatens to discard exactly what matters.
- **In the essay:** Section 9, "Man or machine." Anchor: "human cognition excels precisely because of its limitations"
- **Stands alone because:** It is a short statement of Griggs's broader legibility critique applied to the mind, and it answers "why preserve human cognition?"
- **Micro-article:** "Why Human Cognition Is Worth Preserving: The Case for Messy Minds" · "Is human thinking better than machine thinking in any way?" ·
  - Human cognition is enigmatic and limited; machine cognition is legible and boundless
  - We tend to replace the messy with the legible
  - Paradox as the source of value; caution about trying to beat natural selection
- **Prior art to engage:** James C. Scott, *Seeing Like a State* (legibility; not cited in the essay); Iain McGilchrist, *The Master and His Emissary* (not cited)
- **Connections:** the-question-concerning-digital-technology, the-high-dimensional-society, progress-towards-what, the-reverse-turing-test, can-technology-be-beautiful
- **Strength:** 3

## A right to opt out without opting out of society
- **Type:** design principle
- **Claim:** Griggs holds that societies must make declining cognitive enhancement a real choice. If keeping natural cognition costs too much socially or economically, the choice will have been handed to the market and the state.
- **In the essay:** End of section 9. Anchor: "we will have ceded our agency to preserve human cognition to the logic of the market and the state."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a concrete, rights-based policy principle with clear uses in neurorights debates.
- **Micro-article:** "The Right to Remain Unenhanced" · "Should people have the right to refuse brain enhancement?" ·
  - Why the pressure to enhance will be economic, not legal
  - A choice that costs your place in society isn't a choice
  - Designing systems that keep opting out viable
- **Prior art to engage:** the neurorights movement (Rafael Yuste; Chile's constitutional amendment); cognitive liberty (Nita Farahany) (none cited in the essay)
- **Connections:** manifesto, a-constraint-theory-of-technology (individual right of constraint)
- **Strength:** 3

## "Enhancement" depends on context: who benefits?
- **Type:** critique
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that nothing is an enhancement in itself. It counts as one only relative to a goal that may be chosen or imposed, and those goals are set mostly by the market and the state, so the promises about health are often "opening moves" and even greater intelligence can help an employer while harming the employee.
- **In the essay:** Section 10, "Context Matters," the essay's closing argument. Anchor: "We will only judge an enhancement as “good” if we think that goal is worth pursuing."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a clear conceptual point that applies to any "enhancement" or "productivity" technology, AI copilots included.
- **Micro-article:** "What Makes an Enhancement an Enhancement?" · "Is cognitive enhancement actually good for people?" ·
  - "Better" always means better at some task or goal
  - Health promises as opening moves; adoption driven by monopoly and profit
  - Ask what context the performance is good for, and who benefits
- **Prior art to engage:** the therapy/enhancement distinction in bioethics; Sandel; Altman's "The Merge" and Buterin's d/acc (footnoted)
- **Connections:** a-constraint-theory-of-technology (the market as constraint), the-price-of-innovation, progress-towards-what
- **Strength:** 3
