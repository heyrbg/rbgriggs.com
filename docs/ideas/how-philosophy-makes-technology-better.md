# Ideas in "How Philosophy Makes Technology Better"

The second essay in the philosophy-of-technology series, a list of eight practical benefits philosophy offers technology, aimed partly at skeptical engineers. Most sections carry a standalone idea; it holds 10 distinct ideas.

## Human concepts break when applied to AI; we need new vocabularies
- **Type:** argument
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that concepts like "intelligence," "consciousness," and "creativity" are understood intuitively through human experience, so applying them to machines produces confused reactions, and the variety of machine behavior requires new, granular vocabularies.
- **In the essay:** "Clarify our concepts" section. Anchor: "The sheer variety of intelligence and behavior that we can expect from machines will stretch these definitions beyond their breaking points."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a clear thesis about conceptual confusion in AI discourse, explaining both dismissal of non-humanlike intelligence and over-attribution of personhood.
- **Micro-article:** "Why 'Intelligence' Fails Us When Talking About AI" · "Is it accurate to call AI intelligent or conscious?" · 
  - Intuitive vs. precise concepts
  - Two failure modes: rejecting non-human intelligence, granting chatbots personhood
  - The case for new vocabularies
- **Prior art to engage:** Wittgenstein on family resemblance; conceptual engineering (Cappelen, Chalmers); Murray Shanahan on talking about LLMs (not cited in the essay).
- **Connections:** moral-natures-of-humans-and-machines (the "confusion tax"), schrodingers-chatbot, the-reverse-turing-test
- **Strength:** 2

## Digital life demands personal virtue: attention, relationships, truth
- **Type:** argument
- **Claim:** Griggs holds that digital technology places an under-appreciated burden of personal virtue on individuals to protect attention, relationships, and truth against systems optimized otherwise, a burden too great for almost everyone without philosophical help.
- **In the essay:** "Help our inner philosophers" section, with three developed examples. Anchor: "it can feel like we need the willpower of Gandhi and a PhD in philosophy if we want any chance of getting through this technological world with our sanity intact."
- **Stands alone because:** It frames digital wellbeing as a virtue-ethics problem with an asymmetric fight (individual vs. "armies of engineers").
- **Micro-article:** "The Virtue Tax of Digital Life" · "Why is it so hard to maintain healthy habits with technology?" · 
  - Without practices we accept what each technology optimizes
  - Three fronts: attention, relationships, truth
  - The missing "user manual for digital life"
- **Prior art to engage:** Shannon Vallor's "Technology and the Virtues"; Tristan Harris and the attention economy; Albert Borgmann's focal practices; Cal Newport (not cited in the essay).
- **Connections:** what-does-a-good-digital-life-look, homo-digitalis
- **Strength:** 3

## Philosophy is thinking in advance: predicting the human response
- **Type:** reframe
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that while specific innovation paths are unpredictable, broad trends are predictable, and the real power of prediction lies in anticipating the human response to technology, which can help slow democratic governance keep pace.
- **In the essay:** "Provide a map for the unknown" section, with the Gretzky analogy. Anchor: "At its best, **philosophy is thinking in advance**."
- **Stands alone because:** It defines a practical role for philosophy (anticipatory governance) and shifts forecasting from technology to humans.
- **Micro-article:** "Philosophy as Thinking in Advance" · "Can we predict the social impact of new technologies?" · 
  - Specific paths unpredictable, trends predictable
  - Predict humans, not machines
  - Helping democracy skate to where the puck is going
- **Prior art to engage:** anticipatory governance (Guston); Collingridge dilemma; futures studies (not cited in the essay).
- **Connections:** our-neo-romantic-rebellion (predictions should focus on humans), calling-all-philosophers
- **Strength:** 2

## The missing middle of AI discourse: between legible ethics and sci-fi
- **Type:** critique
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that AI discourse clusters at two extremes, "legible ethics" made salient by political and academic attention (e.g., bias) and quasi-religious doomer/techno-optimist scenarios, leaving a neglected middle about how AI alters what it means to be human.
- **In the essay:** "Elevate the discourse" section, using AI alignment as the example. Anchor: "In between these extremes is a huge gap where vital topics get lost."
- **Stands alone because:** The coinage "legible ethics" and the two-extremes map are portable diagnostics for any AI debate.
- **Micro-article:** "The Missing Middle in the AI Debate" · "What important AI issues are being overlooked between bias and doom?" · 
  - Extreme 1: legible ethics
  - Extreme 2: apocalypse vs. salvation narratives
  - The middle: meaning, interaction, purpose; why extremes are easier to reason about
- **Prior art to engage:** AI ethics vs. AI safety split; Kate Crawford; Marc Andreessen's manifesto and doomerism (linked); Brian Christian "The Alignment Problem" (not cited).
- **Connections:** the-plurality-a-better-myth-for-ai, calling-all-philosophers (dysfunctional discourse)
- **Strength:** 3

## AI forces a reckoning with the void left by the death of God
- **Type:** theory
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that AI will disrupt the Enlightenment values (rationality, symbolic language, sovereign agency) that replaced shared religion as the basis of human specialness, resurrecting the question of what, if anything, is sacred about the human, which makes theology newly relevant.
- **In the essay:** "Bring back theology" section. Anchor: "it will finally force us to confront the void that religion once filled."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a distinctive historical thesis linking secularization, Enlightenment humanism, and AI.
- **Micro-article:** "AI and the Second Death of God" · "Why does AI raise religious questions about human uniqueness?" · 
  - Loss of shared sacred consensus
  - Enlightenment substitutes and how AI undermines each
  - Why theologians have the deepest resources here
- **Prior art to engage:** Nietzsche (alluded to via "the death of God"); Charles Taylor's "A Secular Age"; Harari's "Homo Deus"; Pope Francis/Rome Call for AI Ethics (not cited).
- **Connections:** towards-a-philosophy-of-technology (death of god as forcing function), our-neo-romantic-rebellion, life-is-special-enough, the-case-for-immaterial-progress
- **Strength:** 3

## AI is a new ontology of technology: an agent, not a tool or machine
- **Type:** concept
- **Claim:** Griggs holds that AI introduces an unprecedented ontological category of technology, an agent increasingly capable of acting autonomously, so assuming we'll "figure out AI" like past technologies is unwise.
- **In the essay:** "Cultivate technological wisdom" section, plus the "Provide a map" questions. Anchor: "not a tool, nor a machine, but an agent—one increasingly capable of acting autonomously."
- **Stands alone because:** The tool/machine/agent progression is a citable framework for why historical analogies to AI fail.
- **Micro-article:** "Tool, Machine, Agent: Why AI Has No Historical Precedent" · "Is AI just another tool like past technologies?" · 
  - The three ontologies
  - Why historical analogies (printing press, electricity) break
  - Implications for governance
- **Prior art to engage:** Heidegger on tools; Latour's actor-network theory; Floridi on artificial agency; Simondon (not cited in the essay).
- **Connections:** moral-natures-of-humans-and-machines, the-price-of-innovation, a-constraint-theory-of-technology
- **Strength:** 2

## Some technologies break the trial-and-error model
- **Type:** argument
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that technology usually integrates through trial-and-error, but technologies like AI may break that model because one cannot safely run a trial when the consequences of error are unacceptable, requiring a deeper understanding of trial-and-error itself.
- **In the essay:** "Cultivate technological wisdom" section, briefly. Anchor: "How do you safely perform a trial when the consequences of any error would be beyond acceptable?"
- **Stands alone because:** It is a sharp epistemic problem for innovation policy that recurs across Griggs's work.
- **Micro-article:** "When Trial-and-Error Stops Working" · "Why can't we just learn from AI's mistakes as we go?" · 
  - Trial-and-error as how technologies find fit
  - Unacceptable errors and unrunnable trials
  - Updating practice: what replaces it
- **Prior art to engage:** Popper's piecemeal engineering; Taleb on ruin; Bostrom's existential risk; precautionary principle debates (not cited in the essay).
- **Connections:** our-planetary-predicament ("makes trial-and-error impossible"), manifesto, a-constraint-theory-of-technology, moral-natures-of-humans-and-machines, towards-a-philosophy-of-technology
- **Strength:** 2

## Motivations shape impact: greed and fear externalize costs
- **Type:** critique
- **Claim:** Griggs holds that the motivating forces behind innovation affect its impact, and that market and state, driven primarily by greed and fear, discount downstream effects, sacrifice long-term sustainability, and externalize costs, so second-order effects belong in any wise accounting.
- **In the essay:** "Cultivate technological wisdom" section, one paragraph. Anchor: "The market and the state are primarily motivated by greed and fear, which can discount downstream effects."
- **Stands alone because:** It names a causal link between the motive of innovation and the character of its consequences.
- **Micro-article:** "Greed, Fear, and the Second-Order Effects of Technology" · "Why do new technologies have so many unintended consequences?" · 
  - The law of unintended consequences (popup ad, social network regrets)
  - Market greed and state fear as motivations
  - Accounting for second-order effects
- **Prior art to engage:** Merton on unintended consequences; Pigou on externalities; Shoshana Zuboff; Ethan Zuckerman's "original sin" essay (linked) (mostly not cited).
- **Connections:** a-constraint-theory-of-technology, calling-all-philosophers (default theory), the-price-of-innovation, manifesto
- **Strength:** 2

## The paradoxes of our technological condition
- **Type:** concept
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that no simple good/bad or optimist/pessimist view of technology holds, because our relationship to it is paradoxical: we love and hate it, gain freedom yet have little choice, believe we control it yet it gains autonomy that contests our agency.
- **In the essay:** "Unveil the true face of technology" section. Anchor: "We relish the freedom that technology provides, yet resent how little choice we have to exercise those freedoms."
- **Stands alone because:** It offers a set of named paradoxes as a corrective to polarized tech attitudes; the "could we turn off the Internet?" question is especially citable.
- **Micro-article:** "Can We Turn Off the Internet? The Paradoxes of Technological Autonomy" · "Are humans really in control of technology?" · 
  - Love/hate, capability/diminishment
  - Freedom vs. uniform affordances
  - Control vs. autonomous technology (the Internet, and AI next)
- **Prior art to engage:** Langdon Winner's "Autonomous Technology"; Ellul; Kevin Kelly's "What Technology Wants" (not cited in the essay).
- **Connections:** the-question-concerning-digital-technology, a-constraint-theory-of-technology
- **Strength:** 2

## We live in a techno-human symbiosis
- **Type:** concept
- **Claim:** Griggs holds that technology is an extension of humanity embedded in everything we do, and that dependence runs both ways, since very few could survive months without technology, making ours a techno-human symbiotic world.
- **In the essay:** End of "Unveil the true face of technology." Anchor: "We live in a techno-human symbiotic world."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a compact ontological claim, developed further elsewhere in the corpus.
- **Micro-article:** "Techno-Human Symbiosis" · "Do humans depend on technology as much as technology depends on us?" · 
  - Technology as extension of humanity
  - Mutual dependence
  - Implications for "unplugging" fantasies
- **Prior art to engage:** McLuhan's extensions of man; Andy Clark's "Natural-Born Cyborgs"; Bernard Stiegler on originary technicity (not cited in the essay).
- **Connections:** the-question-concerning-digital-technology (symbiotic relationship), homo-digitalis
- **Strength:** 1
