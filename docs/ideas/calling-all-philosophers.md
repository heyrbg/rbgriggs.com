# Ideas in "Calling All Philosophers..."

A call to action, the first in a series on the philosophy of technology, arguing that philosophers must engage with technology because technology collapses theory into practice. It holds 8 distinct ideas, from a definition of technology to a worked policy example (data coalitions vs. UBI).

## Technology collapses the theory/practice dichotomy
- **Type:** argument
- **Claim:** Griggs holds that separating theoretical from practical questions about technology is a false dichotomy, because technology blurs the two, and that this disconnect is why technological discourse feels superficial.
- **In the essay:** The central thesis, stated early and developed throughout. Anchor: "My contention is that this is a false dichotomy. Technology blurs the distinction between theory and practice."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a general claim about the nature of technological questions that needs no reference to the series or its call to philosophers.
- **Micro-article:** "Why Technology Erases the Line Between Theory and Practice" · "Are questions about AI and technology practical or philosophical?" · 
  - Practical tech questions (AI accountability, geo-engineering, autonomous vehicle harm) hide philosophical cores
  - Why treating theory and practice as separate yields incomplete stances
  - What a reconnected approach looks like
- **Prior art to engage:** Aristotle on theoria/praxis/techne; Marx's eleventh thesis on Feuerbach; Dewey's pragmatism; Carl Mitcham's philosophy of technology (none cited in the essay).
- **Connections:** how-philosophy-makes-technology-better (explicitly framed as the "practice" follow-up), towards-a-philosophy-of-technology
- **Strength:** 3

## Technological questions are philosophical questions in disguise
- **Type:** reframe
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that because every real technological question involves novelty for which no data yet exists, answering it requires imagining possible worlds and preferring one, which makes it normative and not merely empirical.
- **In the essay:** Introduced as "Step one" and argued through the oracle thought experiment. Anchor: "Step one is realizing that technological questions are just philosophical questions in disguise."
- **Stands alone because:** The novelty-to-normativity argument is a compact, portable reasoning chain applicable to any emerging technology.
- **Micro-article:** "Every Tech Policy Question Is a Philosophy Question" · "Why can't data alone tell us how to regulate new technology?" · 
  - Novelty means data is always incomplete
  - Speculation about possible worlds is unavoidable
  - Preferring one world over another is a normative claim requiring a theory of the good
- **Prior art to engage:** Hume's is/ought gap; Collingridge dilemma; anticipatory technology ethics (Brey); possible-worlds reasoning (not cited in the essay).
- **Connections:** progress-towards-what ("can" vs. "ought"), how-philosophy-makes-technology-better ("thinking in advance")
- **Strength:** 3

## The technological oracle thought experiment
- **Type:** thought experiment
- **Claim:** Griggs imagines an oracle whose technological counsel becomes universal policy, showing that answering tech questions forces one through data, speculation, normative theory, practice, and finally generalized principles, which amounts to building a philosophy of technology.
- **In the essay:** A full section walking the reader step by step. Anchor: "Congratulations! You have just created a new philosophy of technology."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a self-contained pedagogical device that explains what a philosophy of technology is and how one is constructed from practice upward.
- **Micro-article:** "The Oracle Test: How to Build a Philosophy of Technology" · "What is a philosophy of technology and how would you create one?" · 
  - The setup: absolute authority over tech policy
  - The steps: data, speculation, normative theory, practice, principles
  - Why the end result is a philosophy, not a policy list
- **Prior art to engage:** Rawls's original position and reflective equilibrium (structurally similar, not cited in the essay); case-based reasoning in common law.
- **Connections:** towards-a-philosophy-of-technology
- **Strength:** 2

## Technology defined as the art of turning theory into practice
- **Type:** concept
- **Claim:** Griggs proposes that technology can be defined as the art of transforming a theory of how the world could be better into practice, defining some version of the good and trying to make it real.
- **In the essay:** A short definitional passage. Anchor: "one way to define technology is the art of transforming theory into practice."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a crisp, citable definition of technology that embeds normativity into the concept itself.
- **Micro-article:** "Technology as Applied Theories of the Good" · "What is technology, philosophically speaking?" · 
  - The definition and its two halves (theory = vision of better; practice = building)
  - Why unforeseeable consequences don't undo the definition
  - Contrast with instrumental and neutral definitions of technology
- **Prior art to engage:** Heidegger's "The Question Concerning Technology"; instrumental vs. substantive theories (Feenberg); value-sensitive design (not cited in the essay).
- **Connections:** the-question-concerning-digital-technology, a-constraint-theory-of-technology, towards-a-philosophy-of-technology
- **Strength:** 2

## The invisible "default theory" of market and state
- **Type:** critique
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that even technologies built only to meet economic or government needs rest on a default theory, that the current world is the best we can do and innovation is driven by market or state, and that its unchallenged dominance makes theory vanish from discourse.
- **In the essay:** Developed over two paragraphs. Anchor: "It just happens to be the _default_ one: the theory that says our current world is the best we can do, where innovation is largely driven by the market or the state."
- **Stands alone because:** It names a hidden ideology in "neutral" engineering and explains why engineers focus on "how" to the exclusion of "why."
- **Micro-article:** "The Default Theory Behind 'Neutral' Technology" · "Is technology development ideologically neutral?" · 
  - Every technology serves some theory, even if unstated
  - The market/state default and its disappearance from view
  - Consequence: practice without "why"
- **Prior art to engage:** Ellul's technique; Marcuse's one-dimensional society; Fisher's capitalist realism; Feenberg's critical theory of technology (not cited in the essay).
- **Connections:** a-constraint-theory-of-technology (market and state as constraints), manifesto, how-philosophy-makes-technology-better (greed and fear as motivations)
- **Strength:** 3

## Why tech discourse is dysfunctional: practice without theory, theory without practice
- **Type:** critique
- **Claim:** Griggs diagnoses technological discourse as split between practical demands that ignore the realities of the default theory and reactionary new theories that lack practical grounding, both of which become shallow, marginal or fantastic, and capturable by culture wars.
- **In the essay:** One paragraph with concrete examples. Anchor: "When practical proposals are disconnected from larger theories, they become reactive and shallow."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a portable diagnostic for evaluating any tech debate (regulation slogans, crypto utopianism, accelerationism).
- **Micro-article:** "Why Debates About Technology Go Nowhere" · "Why does public debate about tech regulation feel so shallow?" · 
  - Failure mode 1: practical demands ungrounded in theory ("more regulation!")
  - Failure mode 2: grand theories ungrounded in practice ("crypto will save us!")
  - The test: can a philosophy make prescriptions logically tied to its theory?
- **Prior art to engage:** not cited in the essay; relates to Winner's "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" and debates over techlash discourse.
- **Connections:** how-philosophy-makes-technology-better ("Elevate the discourse")
- **Strength:** 2

## Public data coalitions as an agency-preserving alternative to UBI
- **Type:** design principle
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that a theory prioritizing human agency and sovereignty reveals UBI's risk of income dependency on government, and suggests public data coalitions that license training data to AI companies, distribute fees, impose ethical terms, and possibly grant citizens equity and governance rights.
- **In the essay:** The essay's worked example, developed over several paragraphs. Anchor: "Now citizens are owners, incentivized to align with AI companies to use that data both ethically and profitably."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a concrete, debatable policy proposal for AI-driven inequality that stands without the meta-argument.
- **Micro-article:** "Data Coalitions: An Alternative to Universal Basic Income in the Age of AI" · "What are alternatives to UBI for sharing AI's economic gains?" · 
  - The AI inequality problem and the UBI response
  - The agency critique: dependency changes the citizen-state relationship
  - Data coalition mechanics: licensing fees, ethical restrictions, equity and governance
- **Prior art to engage:** Jaron Lanier and Glen Weyl's "data dignity" and data unions; Radical Markets (Posner & Weyl); data trusts (Delacroix & Lawrence); Alaska Permanent Fund (not cited in the essay).
- **Connections:** our-neo-romantic-rebellion (data coalitions reappear in the fiction), a-constraint-theory-of-technology
- **Strength:** 3

## Technology as a forcing function for philosophy
- **Type:** argument
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that technology forces philosophy to overcome three challenges, producing theories big enough to answer hard questions, connecting theory to practice beyond quarantined thought experiments, and accounting for innovation's uncertainty, or else technology will answer the big questions for us.
- **In the essay:** The closing section lists the three challenges. Anchor: "If philosophy can’t help answer the big questions, technology is going to answer them for us, whether we like those answers or not."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a challenge to academic philosophy itself that can be argued independently, including the critique that thought experiments are "quarantined from the messy complexity of reality."
- **Micro-article:** "Technology Won't Wait for Philosophy" · "Why should philosophers care about technology now?" · 
  - Urgency: answers needed now, not eternal inquiry
  - The three challenges philosophy must meet
  - The default outcome if it fails: technology decides
- **Prior art to engage:** the "empirical turn" in philosophy of technology (Achterhuis, Verbeek); critiques of trolley-problem ethics in AV design (not cited in the essay).
- **Connections:** towards-a-philosophy-of-technology (also uses "forcing function"), how-philosophy-makes-technology-better
- **Strength:** 2
