# Ideas in "A Constraint Theory of Technology"

Griggs's most systematic essay. It recasts the optimist/pessimist debate as a disagreement about constraints, builds a theory of enabling constraints, diagnoses the market's limits, and proposes eight "constraint-first" policy directions. It holds 15 distinct ideas.

## The optimist/pessimist divide is about constraints, not technology
- **Type:** reframe
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that techno-optimists and techno-pessimists rarely disagree about particular technologies. They disagree about how society chooses and adopts technology, that is, about what constrains it, and pessimism is really distrust of our ability to steward technology.
- **In the essay:** The opening sections. Anchor: "In other words, the difference seems to be about what **constrains** technology."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a clean diagnosis of a polarized public debate that people often ask about.
- **Micro-article:** "What Techno-Optimists and Pessimists Actually Disagree About" · "What is the difference between a techno-optimist and a techno-pessimist?" ·
  - Manifestos rarely mention specific technologies
  - Pessimists distrust our current constraints, not technology as such
  - Optimists mistake constraints for mere limits
- **Prior art to engage:** Marc Andreessen's "Techno-Optimist Manifesto" (linked); the critique piece linked; Langdon Winner (not cited)
- **Connections:** manifesto, how-philosophy-makes-technology-better, the-price-of-innovation
- **Strength:** 3

## Constraints both limit and enable: the core of constraint theory
- **Type:** theory
- **Claim:** Griggs defines constraints as everything that limits and enables innovation (incentives, regulations, norms, resources, status, existing technologies). A constraint's proper job is to find the limit that maximizes the space of possibility, which it does by narrowing quantity to make room for quality.
- **In the essay:** The thesis statement and the end of "The paradox of freedom." Anchor: "Constraints narrow quantity to make more quality possible."
- **Stands alone because:** It is the named theory, and it can be cited on its own as "the constraint theory of technology."
- **Micro-article:** "What Is the Constraint Theory of Technology?" · "What is R.B. Griggs's constraint theory of technology?" ·
  - Definition: constraints limit and enable, and come from the market, state, culture, religion, planet and individuals
  - A constraint's job: the right limit that maximizes possibility
  - Every constraint is a balance between too little and too much limitation
- **Prior art to engage:** Alicia Juarrero's enabling constraints, Terrence Deacon's constraints in emergence (neither cited); Lessig's four modalities of regulation in *Code* (not cited, a close parallel)
- **Connections:** the-case-for-immaterial-progress (technology shapes the constraints on meaning), manifesto, the-high-dimensional-society, the-plurality-a-better-myth-for-ai
- **Strength:** 3

## The paradox of freedom: Chesterton's fence, the haiku, and the conserved genome
- **Type:** argument
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that freedom flourishes only through constraint. A fence at the cliff edge removes one possibility so that a larger space of play opens up. Artistic forms and each brushstroke shape what creativity can do next. Evolution's conserved DNA is what makes variation in the rest adaptive.
- **In the essay:** "The paradox of freedom" section, with three worked examples and a footnote on John Smart's 95/5 rule. Anchor: "It removes one freedom to enable others."
- **Stands alone because:** It is an intuitive, example-led argument that works for any audience and any field.
- **Micro-article:** "The Paradox of Freedom: Why Limits Create Possibility" · "How can constraints increase freedom or creativity?" ·
  - Chesterton's island playground
  - The haiku and the first dab of paint
  - The roughly 5% of human DNA that has been conserved for 200 million years
- **Prior art to engage:** G.K. Chesterton (quoted), John Smart (footnoted); Stravinsky on artistic constraint, Oulipo, Isaiah Berlin's two concepts of liberty (not cited)
- **Connections:** can-technology-be-beautiful, the-majesty-of-language
- **Strength:** 3

## Reactive limits: removing constraints undermines acceleration
- **Type:** argument
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that an unconstrained market doesn't avoid limits but meets them later, as backlash, lawsuits and regulation that are harsher because they are reactive. So accelerationists who strip away constraints undermine acceleration, and providing its own constraints is in the market's interest.
- **In the essay:** The critique of optimists early on, and again in "Convince the market that constraints are a good thing" (red teaming, safe-to-fail probes). Anchor: "So by wanting to remove all constraints, the optimists are actually removing the only effective means of acceleration."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a counterintuitive argument aimed at accelerationists in their own terms.
- **Micro-article:** "Why Accelerationists Should Want Constraints" · "Does regulation slow down innovation?" ·
  - Proactive limits vs. reactive ones
  - Backlash, culture wars and FDA-style mandates as the cost of having no constraints
  - Red teaming as the market constraining itself
- **Prior art to engage:** e/acc; Dave Snowden's Cynefin safe-to-fail probes (linked); OpenAI red-teaming network (linked); the Collingridge dilemma (not cited)
- **Connections:** the-price-of-innovation, manifesto
- **Strength:** 3

## Foundational vs. situational constraints
- **Type:** distinction
- **Claim:** Griggs separates foundational constraints (religion, philosophy, the planet), which are stable, outside technology and big enough to judge it on their own terms, from situational constraints (state, culture, ethics, personal), which respond to change and have more diffuse authority. The foundational ones other than the planet have weakened in a multipolar world.
- **In the essay:** "A brief history of constraints," with examples: Chinese *qi* (via Yuk Hui), cathedrals, the Amish, Greek technē in service of virtue, C.S. Lewis's "Dao," nuclear geopolitics, DARPA, the FDA. Anchor: "They are external to technology and are big enough to judge, guide and evaluate technology on their own terms."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a taxonomy that people can reuse for analyzing technology governance.
- **Micro-article:** "Who Governs Technology? A Taxonomy of Constraints" · "What historically has guided which technologies societies adopt?" ·
  - Foundational: religion, philosophy, planet
  - Situational: state, culture, ethics, the personal
  - Why the loss of foundational constraints leaves a vacuum
- **Prior art to engage:** Yuk Hui's cosmotechnics (footnoted); C.S. Lewis, *The Abolition of Man* (footnoted); Lessig's modalities (not cited)
- **Connections:** progress-towards-what, the-case-for-immaterial-progress, our-planetary-predicament, the-question-concerning-digital-technology
- **Strength:** 2

## The market as innovation idiot-savant
- **Type:** concept
- **Claim:** Griggs portrays the market as the biggest constraint, both foundational and situational. It doesn't care about technology, only about meeting demand more cheaply, so innovation is a side effect. It is wasteful, blind to externalities and blind to values, yet it has produced most technological progress, and its success has drowned out every other constraint.
- **In the essay:** "The market as an innovation idiot savant," which gives the market's case fairly (the invisible hand, the price signal as the closest thing to an objective arbiter). Anchor: "Innovation is a side effect."
- **Stands alone because:** "Idiot-savant" is a memorable, balanced characterization of markets and innovation.
- **Micro-article:** "The Market Is an Innovation Idiot-Savant" · "Why is the free market so good at innovation, and what does it miss?" ·
  - The savant: decentralized coordination and the price signal
  - The idiot: waste, externalities, short time horizons, advertising
  - How success crowded out every other constraint
- **Prior art to engage:** Adam Smith's invisible hand; Hayek on price signals; Schumpeter; Mazzucato's *The Entrepreneurial State* (none cited beyond "invisible hand")
- **Connections:** the-price-of-innovation, towards-a-philosophy-of-technology (note 4), whats-the-deal-with-cognitive-augmentation (context set by market and state)
- **Strength:** 3

## Advanced technology breaks trial and error
- **Type:** argument
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that the market's trial and error works only when trials are iterative, errors show up immediately and a market signal can reverse them. Advanced technologies (leaded gasoline, microplastics, teenage social media) have errors that are slow, catastrophic or second-order, and the market has no interest in running the trials that would catch them.
- **In the essay:** The first of the "market is not big enough" points. Anchor: "Otherwise it turns tragic."
- **Stands alone because:** It is one of Griggs's most recurrent theses, with concrete historical cases.
- **Micro-article:** "Advanced Technology Breaks Trial and Error" · "Why can't we just learn from our mistakes with AI and other powerful technologies?" ·
  - Three conditions under which trial and error works
  - Leaded gasoline, microplastics, a generation's social lives
  - What has to replace it: better trials, broader observation, constraint design
- **Prior art to engage:** the Collingridge dilemma; Taleb on ruin and irreversibility; the precautionary principle (none cited)
- **Connections:** towards-a-philosophy-of-technology, our-planetary-predicament, how-philosophy-makes-technology-better, manifesto, moral-natures-of-humans-and-machines, the-majesty-of-language
- **Strength:** 3

## The market is not big enough
- **Type:** critique
- **Claim:** Griggs argues the market is necessary but not sufficient for advanced technology. It answers to nothing outside itself and can't price flourishing or the moral weight of artificial agents. It hands vital decisions such as AI's direction to a few actors. And it closes off whole areas of technology that would serve flourishing but can't be made profitable.
- **In the essay:** Points 2-4 of "The market is not big enough," with the caveat that these become problems only when the market carries the whole burden of constraint. Anchor: "The market has no capacity to incorporate larger values unless something bigger than the market demands that it does so."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a structured, non-anti-market critique that people can cite in AI governance debates.
- **Micro-article:** "Why the Market Isn't Big Enough to Govern AI" · "Should the free market decide the future of AI?" ·
  - Accountable to nothing outside itself
  - Monopolizes vital decisions (game theory, first-mover advantage, regulatory capture)
  - Closes off unprofitable technologies that would serve flourishing
- **Prior art to engage:** Michael Sandel, *What Money Can't Buy*; Karl Polanyi on embeddedness; Zuboff (not cited)
- **Connections:** towards-a-philosophy-of-technology, manifesto, the-price-of-innovation, the-case-for-immaterial-progress (bringing the immaterial into markets)
- **Strength:** 3

## Constraint-first futures: plan systems, not technologies
- **Type:** design principle
- **Claim:** Griggs holds that we can never plan a future around particular technologies that guarantee flourishing, but we can build systems of constraints that make room for a broad range of outcomes within the bounds of flourishing, so technology policy should start from constraints.
- **In the essay:** "Constraint-first futures" and the conclusion. Anchor: "We need to be thinking “constraint-first” and start enabling a viable system of technological constraints."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a portable method for governing technology that sidesteps utopian and dystopian forecasting.
- **Micro-article:** "Constraint-First: A Better Way to Plan Our Technological Future" · "How should society steer the development of advanced technology?" ·
  - Why planning outcomes around specific technologies fails
  - Planning the constraint system instead
  - Technology and flourishing as co-evolving forces
- **Prior art to engage:** Ostrom's institutional design; Lessig; anticipatory governance (not cited)
- **Connections:** manifesto, the-high-dimensional-society, the-plurality-a-better-myth-for-ai
- **Strength:** 3

## Empower a digital commons to expand the possibility space
- **Type:** design principle
- **Claim:** Griggs proposes that a commons, free from market pressure, is the right home for technologies the market ignores but flourishing needs (civic discourse, privacy and identity, reputation, public knowledge, deliberation), and that it could still turn community values into price signals.
- **In the essay:** First of the constraint-first steps. Anchor: "the commons is a more appropriate container for stewarding technologies that directly impact human well-being"
- **Stands alone because:** It is a concrete institutional proposal with a clear scope.
- **Micro-article:** "Why We Need a Digital Commons for Human Flourishing" · "What technologies would a digital commons build that markets won't?" ·
  - Technologies the market won't touch
  - The commons vs. the state as steward
  - Translating community values into price signals
- **Prior art to engage:** Ostrom's *Governing the Commons*; Yochai Benkler; public digital infrastructure (Ethan Zuckerman) (none cited)
- **Connections:** the-high-dimensional-society, our-planetary-predicament, calling-all-philosophers, our-neo-romantic-rebellion
- **Strength:** 2

## Constraints are themselves a technology: the field of constraint design
- **Type:** concept
- **Claim:** Griggs holds that constraints are a technology, shaped by the constraints they sit within, and calls for a field of "constraint design" with participatory models such as decentralized governance of AI objective functions, stakeholder voting on how much to limit versus enable, and open-source constraint protocols. He adds a reframing of "constraint entrepreneurship," in which building enabling constraints (trial-and-error tools, innovation prediction markets, ethics audits, markets for risk allowances) becomes a richly rewarded industry.
- **In the essay:** "Initiate a new field of constraint design" and "Incentivize constraint entrepreneurship." Anchor: "Constraints are themselves a technology."
- **Stands alone because:** It names a new field and a new industry, which makes it easy to cite and turns governance into something people build.
- **Micro-article:** "Constraint Design: Treating Governance as a Technology" · "Can governance and regulation be designed like technology?" ·
  - Constraints as designed and nested artifacts
  - Participatory, auditable, remixable constraint protocols
  - Constraint entrepreneurship: "an innovation superpower"
- **Prior art to engage:** mechanism design; RegTech; Glen Weyl and Audrey Tang's Plurality (not cited here)
- **Connections:** the-plurality-a-better-myth-for-ai, the-high-dimensional-society, manifesto
- **Strength:** 3

## An individual right of constraint
- **Type:** design principle
- **Claim:** Griggs proposes user-level "constraint customization," such as picking social media algorithms from a public trust, tuning them, copying settings from people you trust, and filtering by polarization thresholds. He argues this would turn technology into a flexible service that respects diverse values and would reduce the need for top-down moderation.
- **In the essay:** "Empower an individual right of constraint." Anchor: "Technology could become a flexible service respecting our diverse values, not a binary take-it-or-leave-it imposition."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a concrete, current proposal relevant to content moderation debates.
- **Micro-article:** "The Right to Constrain Your Own Technology" · "Should users control their own social media algorithms?" ·
  - Algorithm choice from a public trust
  - Value-based routing, polarization filters, attention alerts
  - Less need for top-down moderation and speech restrictions
- **Prior art to engage:** Bluesky's composable moderation and custom feeds; "middleware" (Fukuyama et al.) (not cited)
- **Connections:** whats-the-deal-with-cognitive-augmentation (right to opt out), what-does-a-good-digital-life-look, the-high-dimensional-society
- **Strength:** 2

## Separation of technology and control, against technological apotheosis
- **Type:** design principle
- **Claim:** Griggs proposes enforced checks and balances, modeled on the separation of church and state, such as separating research from commercialization, keeping core protocols in the commons, and splitting development pipelines into modules. The aim is to prevent "technological apotheosis," in which the owners of advanced technology become an autonomous power beyond any institution's control.
- **In the essay:** "Establish 'separation of technology and control'"; the term "technological apotheosis" is coined here. Anchor: "Separation of technology and control prevents any one actor from ever having the possibility of achieving such dominance."
- **Stands alone because:** Both coined terms are memorable and fit AI concentration-of-power concerns.
- **Micro-article:** "Separation of Technology and Control: Preventing Technological Apotheosis" · "How do we stop AI companies from becoming too powerful?" ·
  - The concern: owners whose power becomes autonomous
  - Separation of powers as the model
  - Mechanisms: functional separation, protocols kept in the commons, modular pipelines
- **Prior art to engage:** Montesquieu; antitrust and structural separation (Lina Khan, Tim Wu); AI power-concentration risk literature (none cited)
- **Connections:** manifesto, the-plurality-a-better-myth-for-ai
- **Strength:** 3

## Off-ramps: reversibility enables development
- **Type:** design principle
- **Claim:** Griggs proposes building discontinuation "off-ramps," tripwires, quarantine measures and kill switches into critical technologies, arguing that confidence we can roll a technology back is what makes it safe to develop with confidence.
- **In the essay:** "Enshrine 'off-ramps' into critical systems." Anchor: "The more confident we can be in rolling back a technology in the worst case scenarios, the more confident we can be in developing it."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a pro-development argument for reversibility and a clean example of an enabling constraint.
- **Micro-article:** "Off-Ramps: Why Kill Switches Speed Up Innovation" · "Should AI systems have kill switches?" ·
  - We can't reliably forecast outcomes
  - Pre-agreed tripwires and immune-response plans
  - Reversibility as permission to build
- **Prior art to engage:** AI lab responsible scaling policies; nuclear safety; reversibility in decision theory (not cited)
- **Connections:** the-price-of-innovation
- **Strength:** 2

## The courage not to go there: limits no constraint can hold
- **Type:** argument
- **Claim:** Griggs holds that some technologies (recursively self-improving AI, molecular bio-nanotechnology, merging consciousness with machines) may go beyond anything a constraint can contain. In those cases the only workable constraint may be the courage to refuse, to mark out human boundaries and honor the mystery.
- **In the essay:** "Recognize fundamental 'limits,'" the last of the steps. Anchor: "Accepting that we don’t need to explore every possible future may be the key to ensuring that we have a future at all."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a principled case for technological restraint from an author who is otherwise pro-innovation.
- **Micro-article:** "The Courage Not to Build: When Restraint Is the Only Constraint" · "Are there technologies we should never develop?" ·
  - Technologies that outrun constraint
  - Capability doesn't oblige us to cross every limit
  - Honoring mystery as a constraint
- **Prior art to engage:** Bill Joy, "Why the Future Doesn't Need Us"; Hans Jonas, *The Imperative of Responsibility*; the Asilomar moratorium (none cited)
- **Connections:** whats-the-deal-with-cognitive-augmentation (total merge with machine), life-is-special-enough, towards-a-philosophy-of-technology (note 9 on limits to immortality)
- **Strength:** 2
