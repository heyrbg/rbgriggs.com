# Ideas in "A Neo-Romantic Rebellion"

A playful "weird prediction" that uses Romanticism's revolt against the Enlightenment as a historical parallel for a human revolt against AI, wrapped around a speculative fiction set in 2025 and narrated by a future "we." It holds 7 distinct ideas; the fiction carries serious ones about illegibility, embodiment, and the division of labor between humans and machines.

## Predict human responses, not technologies: humans are weirder than tech
- **Type:** design principle
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that most AI predictions are boring because they focus on what will change about technologies rather than humans, and that history, as the record of weird human responses to novel stimuli, is the better predictive resource.
- **In the essay:** The opening frame and the closing justification. Anchor: "More predictions should recognize that humans will always be weirder than technology."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a methodological principle for forecasting the social impact of any technology.
- **Micro-article:** "Why the Best AI Predictions Are About Humans" · "How should we predict the social effects of AI?" · 
  - The wrong half of the equation
  - History as a database of weird responses
  - Value of improbable predictions for thinking about today
- **Prior art to engage:** Amara's law; Kranzberg's laws; social construction of technology (Pinch & Bijker); Luddite historiography (not cited in the essay).
- **Connections:** how-philosophy-makes-technology-better ("the real power of prediction ... is about the _human response_"), homo-digitalis
- **Strength:** 2

## AI reduces humanity to data as the Enlightenment reduced it to reason
- **Type:** theory
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that AI is not just a technology but, like the Enlightenment, a confrontation with a new vision of the human, one where the human is only what is legible to the machine, making Romanticism the apt historical parallel for our response.
- **In the essay:** The pivot section "How does this relate to AI?" after a substantial history of Romanticism. Anchor: "While the Romantics responded to a vision of humanity reduced to reason, AI is confronting us with a vision of humanity reduced to data."
- **Stands alone because:** It is an original historical analogy with explanatory and predictive force.
- **Micro-article:** "Reason Then, Data Now: AI and the New Romanticism" · "Is there a historical parallel for the backlash against AI?" · 
  - Enlightenment reduction to reason, Romantic revolt
  - AI's reduction to data: culture as commodity, art as training data
  - The instinctive revolt as the seed of a movement
- **Prior art to engage:** Isaiah Berlin's "The Roots of Romanticism" (cited); Luddites; Weber's disenchantment; Kingsnorth and the neo-Luddite turn; "dataism" in Harari (not cited).
- **Connections:** how-philosophy-makes-technology-better (Enlightenment values disrupted by AI), the-case-for-immaterial-progress, life-is-special-enough
- **Strength:** 3

## Romanticism as reaction to the Enlightenment's vision of man, not just industry
- **Type:** reframe
- **Claim:** Griggs holds that Romanticism was not chiefly English poets reacting to the Industrial Revolution but a German-rooted rejection of the Enlightenment's vision of the human, which treated reason and knowledge as prisons and exalted freedom, authenticity, and creation.
- **In the essay:** The long "Wait, what is Romanticism anyway?" section, largely following Berlin. Anchor: "They didn’t see reason and knowledge as keys to universal truths; they saw reason and knowledge as _prisons_."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a concise, opinionated primer on Romanticism framed for technologists, though derivative of Berlin.
- **Micro-article:** "What Romanticism Was Really Rebelling Against" · "What was the Romantic movement a reaction to?" · 
  - Enlightenment exaltation of reason (Descartes, Newton, Kant)
  - French Revolution as reality check; German outsiders
  - Romantic values and legacy: pluralism and the imperfection of all answers
- **Prior art to engage:** Isaiah Berlin (cited and quoted); Charles Taylor's "Sources of the Self"; Hamann and Herder (not cited).
- **Connections:** the-plurality-a-better-myth-for-ai (pluralism)
- **Strength:** 1

## Illegibility as resistance: a language machines cannot learn
- **Type:** thought experiment
- **Claim:** Through the fictional "singslang," a language of clicks, chants, and gestures that requires embodied, relational synchronization so that each communication is unique and untrainable, Griggs imagines illegibility to machines as the core strategy of a human counter-movement.
- **In the essay:** The 2025 fiction, voiced by a future narrator. Anchor: "Could human communication be completely illegible to machines?"
- **Stands alone because:** It is a vivid thought experiment about what remains human if machines can absorb everything recorded; it pairs with real debates over data poisoning and opt-outs.
- **Micro-article:** "Singslang: Could Humans Invent a Language AI Can't Learn?" · "Is there any form of human communication AI can't learn?" · 
  - Legibility as the price of being modeled
  - Embodied, relational, ephemeral meaning as the antidote
  - Echoes: oral tradition, secret guilds, anti-surveillance fashion
- **Prior art to engage:** James C. Scott on legibility and metis; Glaze/Nightshade; Ong's "Orality and Literacy"; McLuhan (mentioned in passing) (mostly not cited).
- **Connections:** the-reverse-turing-test, the-price-of-innovation, whats-the-deal-with-cognitive-augmentation (all discuss legibility); the-high-dimensional-society
- **Strength:** 3

## Embodied coordination as what machines can't replicate
- **Type:** concept
- **Claim:** In the fiction, Griggs makes embodied, in-person, relational synchronization the key property that defeats machine translation, implying that meaning dependent on shared physical presence is a refuge of human uniqueness.
- **In the essay:** Within the singslang passage, briefly. Anchor: "It required _embodied coordination_, where participants synced on a _relational_ key that defined how the different parts of the syntax affected the meaning."
- **Stands alone because:** It isolates a specific mechanism (relational, context-bound keys) that could be argued philosophically outside the fiction.
- **Micro-article:** "Why Embodied Meaning Resists AI" · "What aspects of human communication depend on being physically present?" · 
  - Relational keys and unique communications
  - Ephemerality and the impossibility of training data
  - Connections to enactivism and embodied cognition
- **Prior art to engage:** Merleau-Ponty; Dreyfus's "What Computers Can't Do"; enactivism (Varela, Thompson); Wittgenstein's language games (not cited in the essay).
- **Connections:** the-high-dimensional-society (parts of life we refuse to mediate), the-reverse-turing-test
- **Strength:** 2

## A new division of labor: machines get logic, humans reclaim paradox
- **Type:** prediction
- **Claim:** Voiced by the narrator of the fiction and echoed by Griggs in his own voice, the essay proposes that we will cede logic and calculation to AI and reclaim the paradoxes of the human journey (reason and emotion, conscious and subconscious, animal and divine), the things machines may augment but never replicate.
- **In the essay:** End of the fiction and the closing paragraph. Anchor: "We thank the machines for showing us what we lost."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a hopeful, citable thesis that AI could prompt re-enchantment rather than diminishment.
- **Micro-article:** "Will AI Make Us More Human? The Re-Enchantment Thesis" · "Could AI cause a revival of spirituality, art, and irrationality?" · 
  - What we give the machines
  - What we reclaim: paradoxes, emotion, the sublime
  - Religious resurgence and animism in the fiction
- **Prior art to engage:** Weber's disenchantment and re-enchantment debates; Romantic sublime; Kevin Kelly; Jason Josephson-Storm "The Myth of Disenchantment" (not cited).
- **Connections:** how-philosophy-makes-technology-better (bring back theology), life-is-special-enough, the-case-for-immaterial-progress, towards-a-philosophy-of-technology
- **Strength:** 2

## Weird predictions as a tool for thinking about today
- **Type:** design principle
- **Claim:** Griggs holds that even predictions he admits will not come true, like the Neo-Romantic rebellion, are valuable because they prepare us for the future by improving how we think about the present, and that such a future would hardly be worse than the paperclip apocalypse.
- **In the essay:** The closing, in Griggs's own voice. Anchor: "Will the Neo-Romantic rebellion happen? Of course not."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a short defense of speculative fiction and "weird" forecasting as a philosophical method, and an implicit counter to doom-centered AI imagination.
- **Micro-article:** "The Case for Weird Predictions About AI" · "Why are speculative scenarios useful for thinking about technology?" · 
  - Implausibility as a feature
  - Expanding the space of imagined futures beyond doom
  - Contrast with the paperclip maximizer
- **Prior art to engage:** design fiction (Bruce Sterling); scenario planning (Shell); Bostrom's paperclip maximizer (alluded to) (not cited directly).
- **Connections:** our-planetary-predicament (sci-fi's failure to imagine the near future), the-plurality-a-better-myth-for-ai (myths for AI)
- **Strength:** 1
