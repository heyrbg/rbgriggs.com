# Ideas in "Tech for Life"

The founding manifesto of Tech for Life, arguing that only "life itself" is big enough to guide advanced technology. Behind the rhetorical frame it holds 11 distinct ideas, from a diagnosis of technology's imbalance with the rest of human imagination to a design principle for innovation drawn from evolution.

## Technology is the one dimension of imagination that outgrew the others
- **Type:** theory
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that technology, philosophy, science, art, culture, and politics are all products of human imagination that historically grew together as a whole, but since the industrial revolution technology has grown exponentially while the others stagnated, so technology now escapes the choice those other dimensions once imposed.
- **In the essay:** "A world out of whack", illustrated with three graphs. Anchor: "By achieving increasing powers of causality, technology is escaping the powers of choice that our imaginations were once capable of imposing."
- **Stands alone because:** It recasts the "technology is out of control" worry as an imbalance among human capacities, a model that is easy to cite and visualize.
- **Micro-article:** "Why Technology Feels Out of Control: An Imbalance of Imagination" · "Why does technology seem to dominate everything else in society?" · (1) Imagination's dimensions and their historical unison; (2) The industrial-era divergence; (3) Causality outrunning choice.
- **Prior art to engage:** William Ogburn's cultural lag; Jacques Ellul's autonomous technique; Langdon Winner's *Autonomous Technology*. Not cited in the essay.
- **Connections:** progress-towards-what, the-case-for-immaterial-progress, our-planetary-predicament, homo-digitalis
- **Strength:** 3

## The problem isn't technology; it's that nothing else is big enough to guide it
- **Type:** reframe
- **Claim:** Griggs holds that technology becomes a problem only when nothing else is big or wise enough to guide it, and argues that markets, governments, philosophy and religion, culture and politics, and the "muddle through" status quo each fall short.
- **In the essay:** "Is technology the problem?", with a bulleted list of insufficient guides. Anchor: "the power of our technology only becomes a problem when nothing else is big enough to properly guide it."
- **Stands alone because:** It shifts blame from technology to the institutions around it, and provides a checklist of why each candidate fails for AI alignment and planetary risks.
- **Micro-article:** "Who Should Guide Advanced Technology? Why Markets and Governments Aren't Enough" · "Who should be responsible for steering AI and other powerful technologies?" · (1) Technology as triumph of imagination, not villain; (2) Five insufficient guides and why; (3) The search for something "bigger than technology."
- **Prior art to engage:** Collingridge dilemma; Daniel Schmachtenberger and the metacrisis/multipolar traps; Nick Bostrom's vulnerable world. Not cited in the essay.
- **Connections:** a-constraint-theory-of-technology (market not sufficient to steward technology), our-planetary-predicament, calling-all-philosophers, towards-a-philosophy-of-technology
- **Strength:** 3

## Six symptoms of a world out of balance
- **Type:** concept
- **Claim:** Griggs identifies how technological imbalance feels: technology dominating our imagined future, choices becoming technological demands, a pervasive drive to virtualize everything, innovation motivated only by market greed or state fear, unstoppable technological momentum, and a view of humans as flawed machines to be optimized.
- **In the essay:** "What does a world out of balance feel like?", one paragraph per symptom. Anchor: "To opt out of technology today is to effectively opt out of the economy, governance, and almost all of society."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a diagnostic list readers can recognize in daily life and that maps onto separate critiques (attention, lock-in, incentives).
- **Micro-article:** "Six Signs Technology Has Become a Demand, Not a Choice" · "Why does it feel like we serve technology instead of it serving us?" · (1) Future and attitude (domination, virtualization); (2) Incentives and momentum (greed, fear, lock-in); (3) The human-as-flawed-machine view.
- **Prior art to engage:** Thomas Hughes's technological momentum (term used, not cited); Heidegger's enframing; Postman's *Technopoly*. Not cited in the essay.
- **Connections:** the-question-concerning-digital-technology, the-price-of-innovation, homo-digitalis
- **Strength:** 2

## Life itself as the one thing big enough to guide technology
- **Type:** argument
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that life is the one value all humans share across philosophy, politics, religion, and culture (we want to keep living, want Earth to thrive, want our children's lives to be better), making it the only candidate big enough to unite humanity in guiding technology.
- **In the essay:** "Life itself" section; the manifesto's central thesis. Anchor: "life is the one thing that we can all agree on."
- **Stands alone because:** It proposes a pluralism-compatible foundation for technology ethics, an alternative to contested value lists in AI alignment.
- **Micro-article:** "Life Itself as a Foundation for Technology Ethics" · "What shared value could guide AI and technology for everyone?" · (1) The pluralism problem; (2) Life as the overlapping consensus; (3) What "technology in service of life" means.
- **Prior art to engage:** Hans Jonas's *The Imperative of Responsibility*; Albert Schweitzer's reverence for life; Rawls's overlapping consensus; biophilia (E.O. Wilson). Not cited in the essay.
- **Connections:** life-is-special-enough, our-planetary-predicament, a-constraint-theory-of-technology
- **Strength:** 3

## Life as the universal drive toward wholeness, structure, and integrity
- **Type:** concept
- **Claim:** Griggs adopts a deliberately grand definition of life as a universal drive, stretching back to the Big Bang, toward increasing wholeness, structure, and integrity, continuous from atoms to galaxies to cells to consciousness.
- **In the essay:** "The story of life"; stipulated rather than argued, and combined with the "happened only once" rarity claim from life-is-special-enough. Anchor: "life is that universal drive towards increasing wholeness, structure, and integrity."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a distinctive, cosmological definition that frames technology as continuous with life's process.
- **Micro-article:** "A Cosmic Definition of Life for the Technology Age" · "Is technology part of evolution or separate from it?" · (1) No scientific consensus on life's definition; (2) Life as the drive to wholeness; (3) Technology as life's extension.
- **Prior art to engage:** Teilhard de Chardin; Kevin Kelly's *What Technology Wants* (the technium); Christopher Alexander's wholeness; Schrödinger's *What Is Life?*. Not cited in the essay. Note internal tension: cosmic "life" includes atoms and galaxies while elsewhere life is said to have happened only once on Earth.
- **Connections:** life-is-special-enough, can-technology-be-beautiful, infinite-dimensionality
- **Strength:** 2

## Humanity's foundational duty: stewards of life who can evolve evolution
- **Type:** argument
- **Claim:** Science and technology make humans evolutionary agents able to upgrade evolution from a natural to a conscious, intentional process, which Griggs argues grounds a foundational duty to protect what life has achieved and expand what life can become, since failing it would preclude all other duties.
- **In the essay:** "Humanity's calling as stewards of life". Anchor: "To fail at this duty would preclude the possibility of any other duties."
- **Stands alone because:** It offers a transcendental-style argument for a first duty, applicable to existential risk and space expansion debates.
- **Micro-article:** "The Foundational Duty: Why Stewarding Life Comes First" · "What is humanity's responsibility given the power of modern technology?" · (1) From natural to conscious evolution; (2) The duty to protect and expand; (3) Why it precedes other duties.
- **Prior art to engage:** Hans Jonas ("that there be a humanity"); longtermism (Toby Ord, Will MacAskill); Julian Huxley's evolutionary humanism. Not cited in the essay.
- **Connections:** life-is-special-enough, a-constraint-theory-of-technology, our-planetary-predicament
- **Strength:** 2

## Right relationship: nature as foundation, humanity as steward, technology as extension
- **Type:** distinction
- **Claim:** Griggs defines a right relationship in which nature is the foundation and constraint of life, humanity is life's steward, and technology is life's extension, so technology's purpose is to empower humanity to expand life's integrity and possibility.
- **In the essay:** "Right Relationship" section, a three-part schema. Anchor: "Technology as the extension of life."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a compact answer to "what is technology for?", a question the manifesto says technology cannot answer itself.
- **Micro-article:** "What Is Technology For? A Three-Part Answer" · "What is the purpose of technology?" · (1) Technology is not an end in itself; (2) The nature/humanity/technology schema; (3) Extension, not replacement.
- **Prior art to engage:** Ernst Kapp and McLuhan on technology as extension; Robin Wall Kimmerer and Indigenous "right relationship"; Wendell Berry. Not cited in the essay.
- **Connections:** progress-towards-what, a-constraint-theory-of-technology
- **Strength:** 2

## The more confidently we protect, the more boldly we can experiment
- **Type:** design principle
- **Claim:** From life's principle of adaptation, which balances creation (evolve) and protection (persist), Griggs derives a principle for technology: the more confident we are in protecting what we value, the more experimental we can be with innovation.
- **In the essay:** "Principles of life", the one worked example of translating a life principle into practice, echoed in "Technology in love with life" (conserve the best experiments to run greater ones). Anchor: "The more confident we are in protecting what we ultimately value, the more experimental we can be in pursuing innovation."
- **Stands alone because:** It dissolves the safety-versus-innovation trade-off into a complementarity, which is directly relevant to AI policy debates.
- **Micro-article:** "Protection Enables Innovation: A Lesson From Evolution" · "Do safety and innovation have to be in conflict?" · (1) Life's two drives: evolve and persist; (2) Imbalance either way is fatal; (3) Applying it: invest in testing, measurement, and error correction so innovation can be maximized.
- **Prior art to engage:** Explore/exploit trade-off; evolvability and robustness in biology (Andreas Wagner); James March on exploration and exploitation; Taleb's barbell. Not cited in the essay.
- **Connections:** a-constraint-theory-of-technology (evolution conserves what works, enabling constraints), the-price-of-innovation, the-case-for-immaterial-progress
- **Strength:** 3

## Technology's purpose is to win the infinite game of life
- **Type:** reframe
- **Claim:** Griggs proposes that technology in service of life should aim to keep the game of life going, favoring systems that maximize infinite play over top-down plans or utopias, and improving our methods for adopting technology as much as technology itself.
- **In the essay:** "Technology in love with life", alongside parallel visions of technology in love with humanity (getting out of the way, enriching embodied experience) and nature (progress measured by nature's health). Anchor: "where the best experiment is to keep the experiment going."
- **Stands alone because:** It gives a concrete, non-utopian goal for technology and makes "adoption methods" an object of innovation.
- **Micro-article:** "Technology and the Infinite Game" · "What should be the long-term goal of technological progress?" · (1) Finite versus infinite games; (2) Conserving best experiments to run greater ones; (3) Innovating how we adopt technology, not just what we build.
- **Prior art to engage:** James Carse's *Finite and Infinite Games* (term used, not cited); Karl Popper's piecemeal engineering vs. utopianism; David Deutsch's *The Beginning of Infinity*. Not cited in the essay.
- **Connections:** infinite-dimensionality, the-plurality-a-better-myth-for-ai, progress-towards-what
- **Strength:** 2

## Beyond the natural fallacy: principles of life, not practices of nature
- **Type:** distinction
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that aligning technology with life does not mean treating the natural as good, because evolution keeps evolving; the task is to keep life's broader principles (variation and selection) while finding more efficient and elegant practices than nature's crude reliance on mass pain and death.
- **In the essay:** "None of this will be easy". Anchor: "The principles of life are bigger than any single moment in nature's history."
- **Stands alone because:** It answers the obvious objection to any "life-centered" or nature-aligned ethics and distinguishes principle from mechanism.
- **Micro-article:** "Learning From Nature Without the Naturalistic Fallacy" · "Does aligning technology with nature mean natural is always better?" · (1) The appeal-to-nature objection; (2) Principle (variation and selection) vs. practice (pain and death); (3) Conscious evolution as better implementation.
- **Prior art to engage:** Appeal to nature fallacy and G.E. Moore's naturalistic fallacy; biomimicry (Janine Benyus); David Pearce's abolitionism. Not cited in the essay.
- **Connections:** a-constraint-theory-of-technology, life-is-special-enough
- **Strength:** 2

## You are not a machine; humans as resources for algorithms
- **Type:** critique
- **Claim:** Griggs rejects mechanistic self-descriptions (machine, computer, algorithm, "meat robot," simulation) and argues that technology now reduces humans to resources: training data, content providers, behavioral profiles.
- **In the essay:** Introduction and summary, rhetorical bookends. Anchor: "We are the training data for algorithms, the content providers for recommender engines, the behavioral profiles for advertisers."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a quotable statement of anti-mechanism tied to a concrete account of how platforms use people.
- **Micro-article:** "You Are Not a Machine: Against Computational Metaphors for Humans" · "Are humans just biological computers?" · (1) The machine metaphors and their appeal; (2) Being alive: desire, imagination, meaning; (3) How the metaphor licenses treating people as data.
- **Prior art to engage:** Jaron Lanier's *You Are Not a Gadget*; Heidegger's standing-reserve; Zuboff's surveillance capitalism; W. Brian Arthur (epigraph cited). Others not cited.
- **Connections:** life-is-special-enough, the-question-concerning-digital-technology, the-reverse-turing-test
- **Strength:** 2
