# Ideas in "The Case for (Im)material Progress"

A time-travel thought experiment that turns the "when would you choose to live?" argument for progress on its head, then draws on L.A. Paul, Jonathan Lear and the indigenous critique to argue for a combined (im)material definition of progress. It holds 11 distinct ideas, from a critique of a popular argument to a working definition of immaterial progress.

## Material vs. immaterial progress
- **Type:** distinction
- **Claim:** Griggs holds that progress debates fail because they are about two kinds of progress, material (measurable, the same for everyone) and immaterial (flourishing, meaning, internal and unique), and the two sides speak different languages.
- **In the essay:** Set up in the opening section as the axis dividing "progress defenders" from "progress skeptics"; it frames the whole essay. Anchor: "Immaterial progress is intangible and difficult to measure, based on things that are unique and internal."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a clean, reusable lens for any debate about progress, GDP-beyond metrics, or "are things getting better?"
- **Micro-article:** "Material and Immaterial Progress: Why Progress Debates Talk Past Each Other" · "Why do people disagree about whether the world is getting better?" ·
  - Defenders cite measurable gains (child mortality, literacy, longevity); skeptics sense losses in meaning and purpose
  - Defenders fold the immaterial into individual choice or "standard of living"; skeptics lack a vocabulary for it
  - The fix is a definition of progress that includes both
- **Prior art to engage:** Steven Pinker and Our World in Data-style progress studies, the humanprogress.org list (linked); beyond-GDP / wellbeing economics (not cited in the essay)
- **Connections:** progress-towards-what, the-price-of-innovation, what-does-a-good-digital-life-look
- **Strength:** 3

## The "when would you choose to live?" question smuggles in material values
- **Type:** critique
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that Obama's question (when would you choose to be born if you didn't know who you'd be) does show material progress, but it rigs the answer by treating material progress as the measure of better and worse and projecting that measure backward onto eras with other values.
- **In the essay:** The first section quotes Obama and the Buffett comparison, grants their force, then names the hidden assumption, including the "revealed preference" move of daring the skeptic to pick another era. Anchor: "It posits material progress as the _most important thing_ and projects it backwards in time to make the case for what constitutes “better” and “worse”."
- **Stands alone because:** The veil-of-ignorance argument for progress is common, and a focused rebuttal of it is useful anywhere it appears.
- **Micro-article:** "What Obama's 'Best Time to Be Alive' Question Gets Wrong" · "Is it true that today is the best time in history to be alive?" ·
  - The argument: under a veil of ignorance, everyone picks today
  - The hidden premise: material well-being is what counts, judged by modern standards
  - What it leaves out: the values each era actually lived by
- **Prior art to engage:** Rawls's veil of ignorance (not cited in the essay); Obama's 2017 Goalkeepers speech, Warren Buffett via Collab Fund (cited); revealed-preference reasoning in economics
- **Connections:** progress-towards-what
- **Strength:** 3

## The inverted time-travel thought experiment
- **Type:** thought experiment
- **Claim:** Griggs proposes turning the question around: if a progress defender went back in time and offered people from past eras a place in the present, many would turn it down, because the present has no room for what they value most.
- **In the essay:** Fully staged, with a Ming scholar, an Aztec priest, a 19th-century woodworker, an Edo villager and a 13th-century nun, each of whom asks about values that charts can't answer. Griggs admits the cases are cherry-picked and says the point is that material progress is "incomplete." Anchor: "You have no charts that could speak to these values."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a vivid, memorable device for getting across that progress depends on values, and you can explain it without the rest of the essay.
- **Micro-article:** "Would Anyone From the Past Choose to Live Today?" · "Would people from history actually want to live in the modern world?" ·
  - Setup: the time-traveler's sales pitch (longevity, smartphones, dentistry)
  - The replies: questions about ancestors, the sacred, craft, nature, the divine
  - Lesson: material progress is real but incomplete, and "define your own values" reads to them as strange
- **Prior art to engage:** Thomas Nagel and Bernard Williams on the "relativism of distance" (not cited in the essay); Mark Twain's *A Connecticut Yankee* as a literary precedent (not cited)
- **Connections:** progress-towards-what, our-neo-romantic-rebellion
- **Strength:** 3

## Historical eras are transformative experiences
- **Type:** argument
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that picking a historical era to live in is the same kind of choice as L.A. Paul's vampire case: we picture the past as if we would bring our modern values with us, but anyone raised in that era would hold values we cannot know in advance.
- **In the essay:** A central section applying Paul's *Transformative Experience* directly to Obama's question. Anchor: "as if we’re bringing our modern values and beliefs with us into the past"
- **Stands alone because:** It applies a well-known idea in decision theory to comparing whole civilizations, which is a new use of it.
- **Micro-article:** "Choosing a Century Is Like Becoming a Vampire: L.A. Paul and the Progress Debate" · "How does L.A. Paul's transformative experience apply to judging historical progress?" ·
  - Paul: some experiences change your values, so you can't judge them beforehand
  - Being born into an era is the extreme case
  - So "would you rather live then?" can't be answered the way people assume
- **Prior art to engage:** L.A. Paul, *Transformative Experience* (cited); Edna Ullmann-Margalit on "big decisions" (not cited); Agnes Callard on aspiration (not cited)
- **Connections:** whats-the-deal-with-cognitive-augmentation (enhancement as value-transforming), homo-digitalis
- **Strength:** 3

## The historical clone
- **Type:** thought experiment
- **Claim:** Griggs suggests that even an exact genetic copy of you, raised in another era, would find your values as foreign as anyone else there would, and might live a life full of meaning you can't picture.
- **In the essay:** A short paragraph extending the transformative-experience argument. Anchor: "that **historical clone** would still find your values and beliefs as incomprehensible as anyone else"
- **Stands alone because:** It is a compact way to make the point that values come from history and culture rather than from genes or a fixed self.
- **Micro-article:** "Your Historical Clone: A Thought Experiment on Values and Time" · "Would I have the same values if I had been born in another century?" ·
  - Put your DNA into a historical embryo
  - Upbringing and socialization make it a different person with different values
  - So judging past lives from inside our own values fails
- **Prior art to engage:** nature/nurture debates; Charles Taylor on the "social imaginary" (not cited in the essay)
- **Connections:** homo-digitalis
- **Strength:** 2

## Progress judgments run in both directions
- **Type:** argument
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that anyone from the past could judge our time by their values just as we judge theirs by ours, and would find it disposable, soulless and without anything sacred. So our values can't serve as an objective measuring stick.
- **In the essay:** Its own section, with a list of how the past would indict the present (consumerism, lack of commitment, rituals turned into commodities, disenchantment). Anchor: "This is how the history of progress goes in both directions."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a symmetry argument against presentism, and it applies to any claim of civilizational superiority.
- **Micro-article:** "Progress Goes Both Ways: How the Past Would Judge Us" · "How would people from the past judge modern society?" ·
  - We rightly call the past violent, ignorant and oppressive by our standards
  - The past would call us meaningless, uncommitted and disenchanted by theirs
  - Treating our values as the objective measure can hide their flaws
- **Prior art to engage:** Herbert Butterfield's critique of Whig history; Weber on disenchantment; cultural relativism debates (none cited in the essay)
- **Connections:** our-neo-romantic-rebellion, the-question-concerning-digital-technology
- **Strength:** 3

## Value foreclosure: when a way of life becomes impossible
- **Type:** concept
- **Claim:** Griggs, following Jonathan Lear, holds that historical change can make some values impossible to live out at all, as when Crow courage lost its meaning once the buffalo hunt and intertribal warfare ended, and that this loss counts against calling the change progress.
- **In the essay:** The section on Plenty Coups, built on Lear's *Radical Hope*. Anchor: "Once it was shorn from all tribal context, courage was no longer a meaningful concept that could be enacted."
- **Stands alone because:** The idea that values can be closed off as possibilities, not just weakened, gives a precise way to describe losses that material metrics miss.
- **Micro-article:** "'After This, Nothing Happened': Progress and the Death of Values" · "Can progress destroy values rather than improve them?" ·
  - Plenty Coups and the end of Crow history
  - Values need the right context to be lived; take the context away and they disappear
  - Any accounting of progress has to count values that are lost for good
- **Prior art to engage:** Jonathan Lear, *Radical Hope* (cited); Alasdair MacIntyre on practices and the virtues (not cited)
- **Connections:** the-question-concerning-digital-technology, our-neo-romantic-rebellion
- **Strength:** 3

## Immaterial regression and the indigenous critique
- **Type:** argument
- **Claim:** Griggs observes that Native Americans adopted European technology when it served their own values but would not give up those values for it, and that they saw European society as having gone backward in immaterial terms.
- **In the essay:** Draws on Kondiaronk's critique of money and Benjamin Franklin's report that captives raised among Native Americans refused to return, both via *The Dawn of Everything*. Griggs says plainly that he is not romanticizing either side. Anchor: "they often incorporated technology when it _supported_ their values"
- **Stands alone because:** It is a historical case where people were offered material progress and turned it down, which tests the claim that material progress is what everyone really prefers.
- **Micro-article:** "Immaterial Regression: Why Some Societies Refused 'Progress'" · "Did Native Americans see European civilization as progress?" ·
  - Kondiaronk on money and the "country of money"
  - Franklin's account of captives who refused to come back
  - Technology adopted on a society's own terms, versus values given up for it
- **Prior art to engage:** Graeber and Wengrow, *The Dawn of Everything* (cited); the scholarly dispute over how authentic Kondiaronk's recorded speeches are (noted in a footnote); Amish selective adoption (not cited here)
- **Connections:** a-constraint-theory-of-technology (technology in service of a society's values, e.g. the Amish), our-neo-romantic-rebellion
- **Strength:** 2

## A working definition of immaterial progress
- **Type:** concept
- **Claim:** Griggs defines immaterial progress as a society's growing capacity to explore and engage the widest range of ultimate concerns, individually and collectively, and treats this as a mark of an advanced civilization: Maslow's hierarchy climbed at the level of a whole society.
- **In the essay:** Stated formally in the closing section, drawing on Tillich, with Borgmann's focal practices, eudaimonia and ikigai as examples. Anchor: "The capacity of a society to explore and engage with the broadest range of ultimate concerns, both individually and collectively, in the pursuit of human flourishing."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a concise, quotable definition that someone could build an index or a policy on.
- **Micro-article:** "What Is Immaterial Progress? A Definition" · "How could we measure progress in meaning, not just material wealth?" ·
  - The definition, with Tillich's "ultimate concerns" (not limited to religion)
  - Progress means more people with more resources to pursue what matters most to them
  - Examples: focal practices, eudaimonia, ikigai, even furthering material progress
- **Prior art to engage:** Paul Tillich, Albert Borgmann, Aristotle, Maslow (cited); Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum's capabilities approach (not cited, and a close parallel)
- **Connections:** progress-towards-what, what-does-a-good-digital-life-look
- **Strength:** 3

## (Im)material progress: both/and, not either/or
- **Type:** reframe
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that material and immaterial progress complete each other: material progress without the immaterial can close off possible values, and the immaterial without material progress can't be fully realized.
- **In the essay:** The synthesis that gives the essay its title. Anchor: "Without the immaterial, material progress can foreclose possible values."
- **Stands alone because:** It offers a way out of the optimist/skeptic standoff, and the coined term "(im)material progress" is easy to find in search.
- **Micro-article:** "(Im)material Progress: Uniting the Two Kinds of Progress" · "Is there a way to reconcile progress optimists and skeptics?" ·
  - Each kind of progress needs the other
  - Material gains should serve deeper engagement with what matters
  - What a society aiming at both would reward
- **Prior art to engage:** Progress Studies; degrowth critiques; Borgmann's device paradigm (cited by name only)
- **Connections:** progress-towards-what, a-constraint-theory-of-technology, manifesto
- **Strength:** 3

## Technology shapes the constraints on meaning, and value diversity needs protecting
- **Type:** design principle
- **Claim:** Griggs holds that technology can't create meaning by itself but does shape the constraints and possibilities through which people find it, so a society pursuing (im)material progress would reward innovation that encourages that engagement and would protect a diversity of strongly held values against global homogenization.
- **In the essay:** In the closing description of an (im)material society; briefly stated. Anchor: "It would support efforts to maintain a diversity of values in the face of a globalist, homogenizing, and increasingly connected world."
- **Stands alone because:** It turns the essay's argument into design and policy terms, and it links to Griggs's constraint theory.
- **Micro-article:** "Technology Can't Create Meaning, But It Shapes Where Meaning Is Possible" · "What role does technology play in giving life meaning?" ·
  - Technology sets the constraints and possibilities for finding meaning
  - Encourage innovation that leads to engagement, and bring the immaterial into markets
  - Make room socially for communities with strong internal values
- **Prior art to engage:** Borgmann, *Technology and the Character of Contemporary Life* (linked); value pluralism in Isaiah Berlin (not cited)
- **Connections:** a-constraint-theory-of-technology, the-plurality-a-better-myth-for-ai, the-high-dimensional-society, whats-the-deal-with-cognitive-augmentation (homogenization)
- **Strength:** 2
