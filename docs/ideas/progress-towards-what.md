# Ideas in "Progress Towards What?"

An argument that the stagnation narrative (Gordon's "miracle century," Thiel's flying cars) rests on a backward-looking, measurement-bound myth of progress, organized as four corrections. It holds 9 distinct ideas.

## Growth without a goal is not progress
- **Type:** distinction
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that conflating growth with progress produces a circular definition, where progress is whatever makes the measurements go up, and that genuine progress requires a defined future state judged "better."
- **In the essay:** Section 1, building on the two-part definition of progress. Anchor: "Growth without a goal is not progress."
- **Stands alone because:** The growth/progress distinction is a self-contained conceptual point applicable to economics, tech, and policy.
- **Micro-article:** "Growth Is Not Progress" · "What's the difference between economic growth and progress?" · 
  - Progress requires a destination plus a measure
  - The circularity of growth-defined progress
  - The "miracle century" was declared miraculous only in retrospect
- **Prior art to engage:** Kuznets's warnings on GDP; Stiglitz-Sen-Fitoussi commission; degrowth debates; Progress Studies (Collison & Cowen) (not cited in the essay).
- **Connections:** the-case-for-immaterial-progress, a-constraint-theory-of-technology (market legibility to profit and growth)
- **Strength:** 3

## Measurement-defined progress cannot judge trade-offs about its own measures
- **Type:** argument
- **Claim:** Griggs holds that a notion of progress defined by measurements can only speak to what it measures, so it is silent on questions like how much agency to cede to AI in pursuit of rising incomes.
- **In the essay:** End of section 1, using AI as the example. Anchor: "any version of progress defined by measurements cannot evaluate trade-offs concerning those same measurements."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a sharp logical limitation on metric-driven governance, distinct from the growth/progress point.
- **Micro-article:** "Why Metrics Can't Tell Us How Much Agency to Give AI" · "Can productivity and GDP measure whether AI is progress?" · 
  - What metrics see: jobs, productivity
  - What they can't see: autonomy, agency
  - The self-reference problem: trading off the measure itself
- **Prior art to engage:** Goodhart's law; Campbell's law; James C. Scott's "Seeing Like a State" on legibility; Sen's capabilities approach (not cited in the essay).
- **Connections:** the-high-dimensional-society (proxies collapse reality into metrics), a-constraint-theory-of-technology
- **Strength:** 2

## Progress is the conversion of open problems into solved ones
- **Type:** reframe
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that progress looks forward, so solved problems (like indoor plumbing) drop out of concern and comparing past and present innovations is absurd; true progress makes cross-era comparisons difficult precisely because the current era has been transformed.
- **In the essay:** Section 2, rebutting the "smartphone or toilet?" question and the fire analogy. Anchor: "Progress happens when today’s open problems become yesterday’s solved problems."
- **Stands alone because:** It directly refutes a popular rhetorical move in stagnation debates.
- **Micro-article:** "Would You Trade Your Smartphone for Indoor Plumbing? Why It's the Wrong Question" · "Is modern innovation less important than past inventions like electricity?" · 
  - The toilet-vs-smartphone challenge
  - Progress as a lineage where early impacts may never be matched (fire)
  - Why incomparability is a sign of progress, not stagnation
- **Prior art to engage:** Robert J. Gordon (cited); Tyler Cowen's "The Great Stagnation"; Deutsch's "The Beginning of Infinity" on problem-solving (not cited).
- **Connections:** the-case-for-immaterial-progress (time-travel thought experiment on progress)
- **Strength:** 2

## Bits do things atoms can only dream of: against the atoms/bits stagnation thesis
- **Type:** critique
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that judging digital innovation by its physical-world impact, as in Thiel's "flying cars" complaint, gets progress backwards, because the significance of bits lies in the new worlds of impact they open that previous innovations could not access.
- **In the essay:** Section 2. Anchor: "Bits can do things atoms can only dream of."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a targeted rebuttal to a widely quoted Silicon Valley thesis.
- **Micro-article:** "Flying Cars vs. 140 Characters: Thiel Gets Progress Backwards" · "Did the digital revolution fail to deliver real progress?" · 
  - The atoms/bits distinction and the stagnation thesis
  - Why physical impact is the wrong yardstick for digital tech
  - New domains of impact as the measure of bits
- **Prior art to engage:** Peter Thiel and Founders Fund (cited); Negroponte's "Being Digital"; Gordon (cited); J. Storrs Hall "Where Is My Flying Car?" (not cited).
- **Connections:** the-question-concerning-digital-technology, infinite-dimensionality
- **Strength:** 2

## The "world of beings": progress must account for how technology changes us
- **Type:** concept
- **Claim:** Griggs extends the atoms/bits distinction with a third category, the world of beings, holding that technology reshapes humans themselves and that any notion of progress that cannot account for this is incomplete.
- **In the essay:** Section 3, the essay's most original contribution. Anchor: "Any notion of progress that can’t account for the world of _beings_ is by definition incomplete."
- **Stands alone because:** The atoms/bits/beings triad is a memorable, citable framework.
- **Micro-article:** "Atoms, Bits, and Beings: The Missing Dimension of Progress" · "How does technology change humans, and should that count as progress?" · 
  - Atoms and bits: the standard frame
  - Beings: technology changing identity, values, sense of reality
  - Why progress metrics that ignore beings are incomplete
- **Prior art to engage:** McLuhan (named in passing as "the message is us"); Anton Barba-Kay's "A Web of Our Own Making" (cited in footnote); Sherry Turkle; postphenomenology (not cited).
- **Connections:** homo-digitalis, the-question-concerning-digital-technology, what-does-a-good-digital-life-look
- **Strength:** 3

## Human imagination and coordination, not physics, constrain digital progress
- **Type:** theory
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that since bits face few physical constraints, the main limit on digital innovation is human imagination and capacity to coordinate, so the biggest thing blocking progress may be us rather than science or technology, with crypto as the example.
- **In the essay:** Section 3, with the crypto case (real mathematical breakthroughs, used mostly for fraud and speculation). Anchor: "the biggest thing blocking progress may not be science or technology, but us."
- **Stands alone because:** It relocates the bottleneck of progress from the lab to human coordination, a thesis with wide applicability.
- **Micro-article:** "The Bottleneck of Progress Is Human, Not Technical" · "Why hasn't crypto delivered on its promise?" · 
  - Physical vs. digital constraints
  - Crypto: technical success, human failure
  - Coordination as the ultimate limiting factor
- **Prior art to engage:** Ostrom on collective action; debates on crypto's social failures (Molly White); Ricardo Hausmann on capability (not cited in the essay).
- **Connections:** our-planetary-predicament, manifesto, the-high-dimensional-society, a-constraint-theory-of-technology, towards-a-philosophy-of-technology (all on coordination as the bottleneck); references "our coordination paradox" (not in corpus)
- **Strength:** 3

## Digital natives may have more in common with 1890 than with their grandchildren
- **Type:** prediction
- **Claim:** Griggs suggests that digital technology is reshaping beings so quickly that today's grandparents may have more in common with people raised in the 1890s than with their screen-raised grandchildren, for whom the digital world now feels more "real" than the physical.
- **In the essay:** Section 3, a passing but striking remark, credited partly to Barba-Kay in a footnote. Anchor: "For some generations, the digital world is now what feels “real”."
- **Stands alone because:** It inverts the essay's own opening (1890 vs. 1970) to measure transformation in beings rather than things.
- **Micro-article:** "The Generational Gap Is Bigger Than 1890 to 1970" · "How different are digital natives from previous generations?" · 
  - The opening 1890-1970 story inverted
  - The digital world as malleable, consequence-free space of identity
  - Footnote twist: they might choose smartphones over plumbing
- **Prior art to engage:** Barba-Kay (cited); Jonathan Haidt's "The Anxious Generation"; Jean Twenge (not cited).
- **Connections:** homo-digitalis, what-does-a-good-digital-life-look
- **Strength:** 2

## Progress is a uniquely American myth anchored to a one-time century
- **Type:** critique
- **Claim:** Griggs argues that Gordon's persuasive account of a one-time "miracle century" has leaked into a broader American myth of progress as self-justifying momentum, leaving recent generations with a frozen, backward-looking theory of progress that produces resignation ("the Forever Now").
- **In the essay:** The opening and the Gordon/DeBoer section. Anchor: "Progress itself was never questioned; it was justified by its own indisputable momentum."
- **Stands alone because:** It is a cultural diagnosis of stagnation pessimism separate from the four corrections.
- **Micro-article:** "The Myth of the Miracle Century" · "Why do people believe technological progress has stalled?" · 
  - Gordon's thesis and its legitimate scope
  - How it became a myth of progress
  - The resignation it produces (DeBoer's "Forever Now")
- **Prior art to engage:** Gordon and Freddie DeBoer (cited); Christopher Lasch "The True and Only Heaven"; J.B. Bury "The Idea of Progress" (not cited).
- **Connections:** the-case-for-immaterial-progress
- **Strength:** 2

## Technology tells us what we can do, not what we ought to do
- **Type:** argument
- **Claim:** Griggs holds that defining a better future to progress towards is an inherently philosophical question requiring engagement with religion, politics, and values, because technology expands possibility but cannot supply direction.
- **In the essay:** Section 4, the synthesis. Anchor: "Technology expands the possibilites of what we _can_ do, but it cannot tell us what we _ought_ to do."
- **Stands alone because:** It is the essay's core normative claim and the question "progress towards what?" is a portable slogan.
- **Micro-article:** "Progress Towards What? The Question Technology Can't Answer" · "How should we define progress in an age of AI?" · 
  - Three shifts: measurement to destination, past to future, material to all worlds
  - Why value questions have been excluded from progress talk
  - Why answering them is now necessary
- **Prior art to engage:** Hume's is/ought; Max Weber on value spheres; Progress Studies; Hans Jonas "The Imperative of Responsibility" (not cited in the essay).
- **Connections:** calling-all-philosophers (linked directly), the-case-for-immaterial-progress, towards-a-philosophy-of-technology
- **Strength:** 3
